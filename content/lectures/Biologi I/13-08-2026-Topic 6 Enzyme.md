## Why enzymes matter

Metabolism is the sum of all chemical reactions in a cell. It has two types:

- **Anabolism** — building larger molecules from smaller ones (uses energy).
- **Catabolism** — breaking larger molecules into smaller ones (releases energy, e.g. food → ATP).

Without enzymes, most of these reactions would be far too slow to sustain life. One striking example: a reaction essential to life that takes milliseconds with its enzyme would take an estimated **2.3 billion years** without one.

> **Did you know?** Not all enzymes are proteins — some are made of RNA. These are called **ribozymes**, and they're involved in making RNA and proteins at the ribosome.

## 6.1 Enzymes in metabolic pathways

- Metabolic reactions usually occur in a **sequence** of linked steps — this is a **metabolic pathway**.
- A pathway starts with an initial **reactant/substrate** and ends with a **final product**.
- The product of one step becomes the substrate of the next (these in-between molecules are **intermediates**).
- **Each step is catalyzed by a specific enzyme.**

```
        E1      E2      E3      E4      E5      E6
   A ─────→ B ─────→ C ─────→ D ─────→ E ─────→ F ─────→ G
 (initial              (intermediates)              (end
 reactant)                                          product)
```

Key ideas:

- An **enzyme** is a molecule that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or changed itself.
- Enzymes let reactions happen under mild conditions (normal body temperature/pH) instead of needing extreme heat or pressure.
- Because each enzyme is **specific** to a particular reaction, the set of enzymes present in a cell determines which products that cell can make — this is how cells regulate their own metabolism.

## 6.2 Enzyme structure and function

**Quick recap (protein structure, Topic 2):** amino acids link by peptide bonds into a polypeptide chain (primary structure) → the chain folds into α-helices/β-sheets (secondary structure) → further folding gives the final 3D shape (tertiary structure). Functional proteins built this way include enzymes, antibodies, and motor proteins.

- Only a small part of the enzyme, called the **active site**, actually binds the substrate.
- Enzyme names usually end in **"-ase"** (e.g. amylase, lipase, protease).
- Each enzyme is generally specific to one substrate (or a small group of similar substrates).

### How does the substrate bind to the active site?

| Model | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Lock and key** (older model) | The active site is a rigid shape that fits the substrate exactly, like a key fits a lock. |
| **Induced fit** (current understanding) | The active site is flexible — it slightly changes shape as the substrate binds, molding around it to achieve an optimal fit. |

![Induced fit model of enzyme action — the active site slightly changes shape to fit the substrate, forming an enzyme–substrate complex](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Induced_fit_diagram.svg)
*Image: TimVickers, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**The catalytic cycle:**

1. Substrate binds to the enzyme's active site → forms an **enzyme–substrate (E-S) complex** (a temporary, high-energy "transition state").
2. The substrate is converted — either broken down into smaller products (**degradation**, e.g. digestion) or combined with another substrate into a larger product (**synthesis**, e.g. building a polymer).
3. Products are released.
4. The active site returns to its original shape, ready to bind another substrate molecule.

The enzyme itself is unchanged at the end and can be reused repeatedly.

## 6.3 Enzyme and energy of activation

- Every reaction needs a certain amount of energy input before it can proceed — this is the **activation energy (Eₐ)**.
- Without an enzyme, molecules often need extra energy (e.g. heat) to collide effectively enough to react.
- Enzymes **lower the activation energy** needed, without changing the energy of the reactants or products.

![Activation energy diagram showing how a catalyst/enzyme lowers the energy barrier of a reaction](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Activation_energy.svg)
*Image: based on original by Vinay.bhat, via Wikimedia Commons (free use).*

**How does an enzyme lower Eₐ?**

- Instead of substrates randomly colliding, the enzyme **precisely orients** them within the active site.
- It brings substrates into close contact and aligns them correctly.
- It **stabilizes the transition state** — the unstable, high-energy intermediate stage of the reaction — which reduces the energy needed to get the reaction started.

Net effect: the reaction happens much faster, at the mild temperatures found inside a living cell.

## 6.4 Factors affecting rate of reaction

Rate of reaction = amount of product formed per unit time. Five main factors affect it:

### a. Temperature

![Graph showing enzyme activity rising to an optimum then falling sharply due to denaturation as temperature increases](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Effect_of_temperature_on_enzymes.svg)
*Image: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.*

- **Below optimal temperature:** molecular motion is slow, so enzyme and substrate collide less often → reaction rate is low. The enzyme's structure is still intact.
- **At optimal temperature:** reaction rate is at its **maximum** — enzyme and substrate have just the right kinetic energy for frequent, successful collisions. (Most human enzymes: ~37°C.)
- **Above optimal temperature:** heat breaks the hydrogen bonds and other weak interactions holding the enzyme's 3D shape together. This is **denaturation** — the enzyme loses its shape (especially the active site) and activity drops sharply.
- **Exception:** enzymes from prokaryotes living in extreme heat (e.g. hot springs) — such as some amylase, lipase, protease, xylanase, and DNA polymerase — have optimal activity at 45°C to nearly 100°C and don't denature under conditions that would destroy typical enzymes.

### b. pH

![Graph showing enzyme activity peaking at a specific optimal pH](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Enzyme-pH.svg)
*Image: Wikimedia Commons.*

- Each enzyme has its own **optimal pH**. Examples: salivary amylase (~pH 6.7–7.0), pepsin (~pH 1.5–2.0, active in the stomach), trypsin (~pH 7.8–8.7, active in the small intestine).
- A change in pH alters the concentration of H⁺ ions, which changes the charge of amino acid **R-groups** (side chains) in the enzyme.
- This can change how the protein folds and how the active site behaves — at extreme pH, the enzyme's structure is disrupted and it becomes inactive (similar effect to denaturation).

### c. Substrate concentration

- More substrate → more frequent collisions with the enzyme → faster rate, **up to a point**.
- Once all active sites are continuously occupied, the rate plateaus at the **saturation point** — adding more substrate no longer speeds things up because enzyme (not substrate) is now the limiting factor.

### d. Enzyme concentration

- If substrate is plentiful, more enzyme = more active sites available = faster rate.
- But if substrate is limited, adding more enzyme beyond a certain point has little effect — the rate plateaus because **substrate becomes the limiting factor**.

### e. Cofactors

Many enzymes need a non-protein helper molecule at the active site to function properly, called a **cofactor**.

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Inorganic cofactors** | Metal ions that assist directly in catalysis | Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe³⁺ |
| **Non-protein organic cofactors (coenzymes)** | Carrier molecules that transfer electrons/atoms during the reaction | NAD⁺, FAD, NADP⁺ |

- Coenzymes often come from **vitamins** in our diet — e.g. niacin is part of NAD⁺, and riboflavin (vitamin B₂) is part of FAD. This is why trace vitamin intake is essential even though the body needs only tiny amounts.

## 6.5 Enzyme activation and inhibition

Cells must control **when and where** each enzyme is active. They do this by regulating the presence, concentration, or activity state of enzymes.

### Activation

Enzymes aren't always made in their active form. Ways to switch them on include:

1. **Adding or removing a phosphate group** — a **kinase** adds phosphate (can activate or deactivate an enzyme); a **phosphatase** removes it.
2. **Associating with another protein or cofactor.**

### Inhibition

An **inhibitor** is a molecule that binds to an enzyme and reduces its activity.

| Type | Reversible? | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Competitive inhibition** | Reversible | Inhibitor resembles the substrate and competes for the **active site**. |
| **Non-competitive inhibition** | Reversible | Inhibitor binds an **allosteric site** (a site other than the active site), changing the active site's shape. |
| **Irreversible inhibition** | Permanent | Inhibitor binds and destroys/permanently blocks the active site (often via a covalent bond). |

![Diagram comparing competitive inhibition, non-competitive inhibition, and end-product (feedback) inhibition](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/The_three_types_of_enzyme_inhibition_of_non-competitive_inhibition,_competitive_inhibition,_and_end-product_inhibition.svg)
*Image: Wikimedia Commons.*

**Competitive inhibition**

- Substrate and inhibitor both fit the active site and compete for it.
- Because they're competing for the same site, **increasing substrate concentration can outcompete the inhibitor** — the reaction can still reach its normal maximum rate, just at a higher substrate concentration.
- Example: methotrexate (an anticancer drug) competes with folic acid for the active site of dihydrofolate reductase, slowing cancer cell growth.

**Non-competitive inhibition**

- Inhibitor binds the allosteric site, distorting the active site shape so the substrate can no longer bind properly.
- Since the inhibitor doesn't compete for the active site, adding more substrate **cannot** restore full activity — the reaction's maximum rate is permanently lowered while the inhibitor is present.

**Irreversible inhibition**

- The inhibitor binds permanently (commonly via a covalent bond) at or near the active site and cannot dissociate.
- Example: **DIFP** (diisopropyl fluorophosphate), a nerve gas, irreversibly inhibits **acetylcholinesterase**. This causes acetylcholine to build up, nerve impulses can't be switched off, and prolonged muscle contraction leads to paralysis or death.

**Feedback (end-product) inhibition**

- A special case of non-competitive/allosteric inhibition: the **final product of a pathway** builds up and binds the allosteric site of an earlier enzyme in that same pathway, switching it off.
- This is a **negative feedback loop** — it prevents the cell from wastefully overproducing a product it doesn't need right now.

## 6.6 Importance and application of enzymes

Enzymes are used as **biocatalysts** across many industries:

| Enzymes | Industry | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Protease, lipase, amylase, cellulase | Biological detergents | Stain removal, breaking down fats/oils, color retention |
| Trypsin, amylase, glucose isomerase, papain, pectinase | Food processing | Converting starch to glucose, making high-fructose corn syrup and prebiotics, debittering fruit juice |
| Lipase, cellulase, xylanase | Biofuels | Producing fatty acid methyl esters; breaking down plant material for bioethanol |
| Amylase, protease | Baking | Breaking down starch to maltose, breaking down proteins |
| Phytase | Agriculture | Improving animal feed efficiency |
