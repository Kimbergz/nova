## 7.1 Overview of photosynthesis

**Photosynthesis** is the process that converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose and other organic molecules, releasing oxygen as a byproduct.

- In the presence of light, the green parts of plants (their pigments) produce glucose and O₂ from CO₂ and H₂O.
- Photosynthetic organisms — cyanobacteria, algae, and plants — transform solar energy into the chemical energy of carbohydrates.

**Overall equation:**

$$6CO_2 + 6H_2O \xrightarrow{\text{light}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2$$

Photosynthesis happens in **two linked sets of reactions**:

| Stage | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Light reactions** | Thylakoid membrane | Chlorophyll captures light energy → produces ATP and NADPH → splits water (releases O₂) |
| **Calvin cycle** | Stroma | Uses the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to reduce CO₂ into carbohydrate |

### The site of photosynthesis

In plants, photosynthesis takes place in the **chloroplast**, found mainly in mesophyll cells of the leaf.

![Structure of a chloroplast showing the outer/inner membrane, stroma, thylakoids and grana](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Chloroplast_structure.svg)
*Image: Kelvinsong, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.*

- **Stroma** — the fluid-filled cavity that contains the enzymes for the Calvin cycle.
- **Thylakoid membrane** — folded membrane system holding the photosynthetic pigments (e.g. chlorophyll); this is where the light reactions occur.
- **Stomata** — microscopic pores on the leaf surface where gas exchange (CO₂ in, O₂ out) occurs.

## 7.2 The process of photosynthesis — light reactions

The **light reactions** convert light energy into the chemical energy of ATP and NADPH, releasing oxygen in the process. This is also called **photophosphorylation**. It occurs at the thylakoid membrane and comes in two forms:

- **Non-cyclic photophosphorylation** — uses both Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI); produces ATP, NADPH, **and** O₂.
- **Cyclic photophosphorylation** — uses only Photosystem I (PSI); produces **ATP only**.

### Organization of the thylakoid membrane

![Diagram of the photosynthetic electron transport chain across the thylakoid membrane, showing Photosystem II, the cytochrome complex, Photosystem I, and ATP synthase](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Photosynthetic_electron_transport_chain_EN.svg)
*Image: Chandres, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0.*

| Component | Consists of | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Photosystem II (PSII)** | Pigment complex + electron acceptors | Captures light energy; splits water into O₂, protons (H⁺), and electrons |
| **Electron transport chain (ETC)** | Cytochrome complex + plastoquinone (Pq) | Carries electrons from PSII to PSI; pumps H⁺ from stroma into thylakoid space |
| **Photosystem I (PSI)** | Pigment complex + electron acceptors | Re-energizes electrons; helps reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH |
| **ATP synthase complex** | Channel for H⁺ flow | Uses the H⁺ gradient to make ATP from ADP + Pᵢ |

A **photosystem** is an organized cluster of pigments, proteins, and small organic molecules embedded in the thylakoid membrane, made of two parts:

1. **Reaction center complex** — a pair of chlorophyll *a* molecules plus proteins and a primary electron acceptor.
2. **Light-harvesting complex** — accessory pigments bound to protein; absorbs photons and passes the energy to the reaction center.

### Photosynthetic pigments and the absorption spectrum

Pigments absorb certain wavelengths of visible light and reflect/transmit the rest — this is why we see color. **Chlorophyll** is the most abundant pigment in the thylakoid membrane.

![Absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b across the visible light spectrum](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Chlorophyll_ab_spectra-en.svg)
*Image: Daniele Pugliesi, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.*

- **Chlorophyll** looks green because it absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light.
- **Carotenoids** (accessory pigments) absorb violet-blue-green light and reflect yellow/orange light.

### The role of NADP⁺/NADPH

NADP⁺/NADPH is the redox coenzyme linking the two stages of photosynthesis.

- **In the light reactions:** NADP⁺ acts as an electron acceptor — it accepts 2 electrons and 1 H⁺ to become NADPH.
- **In the Calvin cycle:** NADPH acts as an electron carrier — it donates its electrons (is oxidized) to help build glucose.
  
### Non-cyclic photophosphorylation (step by step)

1. Pigments in **PSII** capture light energy.
2. Energy passes between pigments until it concentrates at the reaction center.
3. Electrons become so energized that one is ejected from the reaction center to a primary electron acceptor.
4. The missing electron in PSII is replaced by an electron from the **splitting of water (photolysis)**, which produces O₂ and H⁺.
5. The ejected electron travels down the **ETC** toward PSI. As it passes from carrier to carrier, its energy is used to actively pump H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space.
6. The buildup of H⁺ in the thylakoid space drives ATP production (see chemiosmosis below).
7. Pigments in **PSI** capture light energy and pass it to the PSI reaction center, where another electron is energized and ejected to a new electron acceptor.
8. The "hole" left in PSI is filled by the electron arriving from PSII via the ETC.
9. The newly ejected electron from PSI is transferred to **NADP⁺**, which — with 2 electrons and 1 H⁺ — becomes **NADPH** (with help from the enzyme NADP⁺ reductase).
10. The ATP and NADPH produced are used in the Calvin cycle.

### ATP production by chemiosmosis

1. The thylakoid space acts as a reservoir for H⁺.
2. Each time water is split (photolysis), H⁺ ions accumulate inside the thylakoid space.
3. Energized electrons moving down the ETC (via plastoquinone → cytochrome complex → plastocyanin) lose energy as they travel.
4. That energy is used to actively pump more H⁺ into the thylakoid space, building a steep **proton gradient**.
5. This whole process — making ATP using an H⁺ gradient — is called **chemiosmosis**.
6. H⁺ flows back out from high to low concentration through **ATP synthase**, and the kinetic energy of that flow drives ATP synthase to combine ADP + Pᵢ into ATP.

### Cyclic photophosphorylation (step by step)

1. Light energy is absorbed by pigments of **PSI** only.
2. The photon's energy passes between pigments until it reaches the PSI reaction center, exciting an electron.
3. The photoexcited electron is ejected to a primary electron acceptor.
4. The electron passes through an ETC made of **ferredoxin (Fd) → cytochrome complex → plastocyanin (Pc)**.
5. Energy released during this transfer is used to pump H⁺ and make **ATP** via chemiosmosis.
6. The electron returns to PSI (a closed loop) instead of going to NADP⁺.
7. Only **ATP** is produced — no NADPH, no O₂ — because water is never split in this pathway.

### Comparing the two pathways

| | Non-cyclic photophosphorylation | Cyclic photophosphorylation |
|---|---|---|
| Electron flow | Linear (not cyclic) | Cyclic |
| Products | ATP + NADPH + O₂ | ATP only |
| Photosystems involved | PSII and PSI | PSI only |
| Electron source | Photolysis of water | Recycled from PSI |
| Final electron acceptor | NADP⁺ | Back to PSI |
| O₂ released? | Yes | No |
| Photolysis of water? | Yes | No |

*Similarities: both make ATP by photophosphorylation, and both occur in the thylakoid membrane during the light reactions.*

## 7.3 Plants fix carbon dioxide — the Calvin cycle

The **Calvin cycle** (also called the **C3 pathway**) is the set of light-*independent* reactions that convert CO₂ from the air into sugar. It occurs in the **stroma**.

- The first stable product formed after carbon fixation is a 3-carbon molecule, **3-phosphoglycerate (PGA)** — hence "C3 pathway."
- It requires: **CO₂** (gets reduced to organic molecules), **ATP** (energy source), and **NADPH** (reducing power/electron source) — both ATP and NADPH come from the light reactions.

![The Calvin cycle: carbon fixation, reduction, and regeneration of RuBP](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Calvin-cycle4.svg)
*Image: Mike Jones (Adenosine), via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.*

Overall equation:

$$12\,NADPH + 18\,ATP + 6\,CO_2 \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + 12\,NADP^+ + 18\,ADP + 18\,P_i + 6\,H_2O$$

The Calvin cycle has **three stages**:

### 1. Carbon dioxide fixation

- 3 molecules of CO₂ bind to 3 molecules of the 5-carbon sugar **ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP)**.
- Catalyzed by the enzyme **RuBisCO** (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase).
- This forms 3 unstable 6-carbon intermediates, which each immediately split into 2 molecules of **3-phosphoglycerate (PGA)** — 6 molecules of PGA in total.

### 2. Carbon dioxide reduction

- The 6 molecules of PGA are phosphorylated using 6 **ATP**, forming 6 molecules of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (BPG).
- The 6 molecules of BPG are then reduced using 6 **NADPH**, forming 6 molecules of **glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P)**.
- 1 of the 6 G3P molecules exits the cycle to be used for making glucose or other organic compounds.
- The remaining 5 G3P molecules stay in the cycle to regenerate RuBP.

### 3. RuBP regeneration

- Through a series of reactions (using 3 more **ATP**), the 5 remaining G3P molecules are rearranged into 3 molecules of RuBP.
- RuBP is now ready to bind another CO₂, and the cycle continues.

**Summary numbers:**

| To make... | You need | Turns of the cycle |
|---|---|---|
| 1 net G3P | 3 CO₂ + 9 ATP + 6 NADPH | 3 turns |
| 1 glucose (= 2 G3P) | 6 CO₂ + 18 ATP + 12 NADPH | 6 turns |

### Fate of G3P

G3P is a versatile 3-carbon building block. Its carbon skeleton can go on to form:

- Fatty acids and glycerol (→ plant oils)
- Glucose phosphate (a simple sugar)
- Fructose (which combines with glucose to form sucrose)
- Starch and cellulose
- Amino acids

## 7.4 Other types of photosynthesis

### C3 plants

Most plants are **C3 plants** — they use the standard Calvin cycle pathway described above, with RuBisCO producing the 3-carbon compound 3-phosphoglycerate as the first product. This works well in cool, moist conditions.

**The problem:** on hot, dry days, C3 plants undergo **photorespiration**, which lowers photosynthetic output.

### Photorespiration

Photorespiration occurs when RuBisCO fixes **O₂** instead of CO₂ — it consumes O₂ and releases CO₂, using ATP but producing less sugar.

Why it happens:

- On hot, dry days, stomata must close to prevent water loss.
- With stomata closed, CO₂ inside the leaf drops and O₂ builds up.
- RuBisCO then has a higher chance of binding O₂ instead of CO₂.
- O₂ combines with RuBP instead of CO₂, producing **phosphoglycolate (2C)** and **phosphoglycerate (3C)** rather than two molecules of the normal 3C product.
- Processing phosphoglycolate uses ATP and releases CO₂ — a net energy loss with no sugar gain.

### C4 and CAM plants — alternative carbon fixation

Both strategies evolved to **minimize photorespiration** by concentrating CO₂ around RuBisCO before the Calvin cycle starts.

| | C4 plants | CAM plants |
|---|---|---|
| How CO₂ fixation is separated | Between **two cell types**: mesophyll and bundle sheath cells | Between **two times of day**: night and day |
| Pathways used | C4 pathway + Calvin cycle | CAM pathway + Calvin cycle |
| Examples | Corn, sugarcane | Cactus, pineapple |

### Leaf structure: C3 vs C4 (Kranz anatomy)

- **C3 leaves:** mesophyll cells arranged in parallel layers.
- **C4 leaves:** mesophyll cells arranged concentrically around the bundle sheath cells (this ring arrangement is called **Kranz anatomy**); both bundle sheath and mesophyll cells contain chloroplasts.

![C4 leaf anatomy showing mesophyll cells arranged around bundle sheath cells (Kranz anatomy)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/C4_Plant_Anatomy.svg)
*Image: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.*

### Carbon fixation in C4 plants

**Step 1 — in the mesophyll cell (first carbon fixation):**

1. CO₂ reacts with **phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)**, a 3C molecule, forming **oxaloacetate (4C)**.
2. Catalyzed by **PEP carboxylase**, which has a much higher affinity for CO₂ than RuBisCO does.
3. Oxaloacetate is reduced by NADPH to **malate (4C)** (NADPH is oxidized to NADP⁺).
4. Malate is transported into the bundle-sheath cells through plasmodesmata.

**Step 2 — in the bundle-sheath cell (second carbon fixation):**

5. Malate is reduced/broken down to **pyruvate (3C)**, releasing CO₂.
6. This released CO₂ enters the **Calvin cycle** as normal (RuBisCO fixes it here, shielded from atmospheric O₂).

### Carbon fixation in CAM plants

**At night:**

1. CO₂ enters the leaf through open stomata.
2. **PEP carboxylase (PEPCase)** catalyzes CO₂ + PEP → oxaloacetate.
3. Oxaloacetate is reduced to **malate (4C)** and stored (typically in the vacuole) overnight.

**During the day:**

- Stomata close to conserve water.
- Stored malate is broken down to release CO₂, which is fixed into RuBP via the **Calvin cycle** using RuBisCO (ATP and NADPH are available from the light reactions, since it's daytime).

### PEP carboxylase vs RuBisCO

| | PEP carboxylase | RuBisCO |
|---|---|---|
| Reaction catalyzed | CO₂ + PEP → oxaloacetate | CO₂ + RuBP → 3-phosphoglycerate |
| Affinity for CO₂ | High, even at low CO₂ concentration | High only when CO₂ > O₂ concentration |
| Affinity for O₂ | None (won't bind O₂) | Binds O₂ when O₂ concentration is higher — causes photorespiration |
| Location in C4 plants | Mesophyll cells | Bundle-sheath cells |
| Role in C4 pathway | Catalyzes the *first* carbon fixation | Catalyzes the *second* fixation, in the Calvin cycle |

### Matching strategy to environment

| Pathway | Most efficient when... | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **C3** | Cool, moist, high CO₂ | High photorespiration in hot conditions |
| **C4** | Hot, dry, low CO₂ | Needs more ATP to make the same glucose |
| **CAM** | Hot, arid/drought regions | Slower growth than C3 or C4 |

### C3 vs C4 vs CAM — quick comparison

| | C3 plants | C4 plants | CAM plants |
|---|---|---|---|
| CO₂ fixation | Once, directly via Calvin cycle | Twice: mesophyll then bundle sheath | Twice: at night, then during the day |
| CO₂ acceptor | RuBP | PEP (first fixation) | PEP (at night) |
| CO₂-fixing enzyme | RuBisCO | PEP carboxylase then RuBisCO | PEP carboxylase then RuBisCO |
| First intermediate product | 3-phosphoglycerate (3C) | Oxaloacetate (4C) | Oxaloacetate (4C) → malate |
| Rate of photorespiration | High (in hot/dry conditions) | Low | Low |
