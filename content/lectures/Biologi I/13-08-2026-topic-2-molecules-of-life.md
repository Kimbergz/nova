## 2.1 Chemistry of Water

**Water is a polar molecule.** Its shape and polarity make **hydrogen bonding** possible.

- **Oxygen** is partially negative (δ⁻).
- **Hydrogens** are partially positive (δ⁺).
- The H–O–H bond angle is **104.5°**.

### The hydrogen bond

A **hydrogen bond** is a **weak attraction** between a slightly positive hydrogen atom and a slightly negative atom.

- It can occur **between different molecules** or **within the same molecule**.
- A **single** hydrogen bond is easily broken, but **many** hydrogen bonds together are quite strong.
- It helps maintain the proper structure and function of complex molecules such as **proteins** and **DNA**.

![Hydrogen bonding between water molecules](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/3D_model_hydrogen_bonds_in_water.svg?width=460)
*Image: "3D model hydrogen bonds in water," Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA).*

### The 5 properties of water

**1. Water has a high heat capacity.**
- The many hydrogen bonds let water absorb a large amount of thermal energy **without a great change in temperature**.
- Water's temperature rises and falls **slowly**.
- This lets organisms keep normal internal temperatures, protected from rapid temperature changes.
- (It takes **80 calories** to freeze 1 g of water and **540 calories** to evaporate 1 g of water.)

**2. Water has a high heat of vaporization.**
- Water is **harder to evaporate** than alcohol because of its higher heat of vaporization.
- **Hydrogen bonds must be broken** to evaporate water.
- Bodies of organisms **cool** when their heat is used to evaporate water — e.g. **sweating** (animals) and **transpiration** (plants).

**3. Water is a good solvent.**
- Because it is polar, water dissolves ionic salts (e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻) and other polar substances.
- Dissolved substances can then be **transported** around the body / cell.

**4. Water molecules are cohesive and adhesive.**
- **Cohesion** — water molecules cling to **each other** (hydrogen bonding). This lets water flow freely and gives **surface tension**.
- **Adhesion** — water molecules cling to **other polar surfaces** (due to water's polarity). This gives **capillary action**.
- Together, cohesion and adhesion account for **water transport in plants** (roots → stems → leaves) and **transport in blood vessels**.

**5. Frozen water (ice) is less dense than liquid water.**
- Below **4 °C**, hydrogen bonds become more rigid but also more **open**.
- Water **expands** as it reaches 0 °C and freezes.
- **Ice floats** on liquid water.
- Without this, oceans would freeze **solid** (from the bottom up) instead of from the top down.
- The ice layer acts as an **insulator** on top of a frozen body of water, letting fish and other life survive below.

## 2.2 Organic Molecules

- **Organic molecules** contain both **carbon** and **hydrogen** atoms.
- Four classes of organic molecules (**biomolecules**) exist in living organisms:
  - **Carbohydrates · Lipids · Proteins · Nucleic Acids**
- Their functions in the cell are diverse.

### Monomers and polymers

- Biomolecules usually consist of many repeating units.
- Each repeating unit is a **monomer**.
- A molecule made of repeating monomers is a **polymer** ("many parts").
- Example: amino acids (monomers) join to form a protein (polymer).

| Category | Subunit (monomer) | Polymer |
| -------- | ----------------- | ------- |
| Carbohydrates | Monosaccharide | Polysaccharide |
| Lipids | Glycerol and fatty acids | Fat |
| Proteins | Amino acids | Polypeptide |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotide | DNA, RNA |

> **Note:** **Lipids are NOT polymers** because they contain **two different types of subunit** (glycerol + fatty acids), not one repeating unit.

### Synthesis and degradation

- **Dehydration reaction (synthesis):** subunits are joined by forming a **covalent bond**, and **water is produced**. Used to build polymers from monomers.
  - *Reactants:* monomers → *Product:* polymer → *Side product:* **water**
  - Example: forming **starch** (polymer) from glucose (monomers).
- **Hydrolysis reaction (degradation):** a **water molecule is added** to break a covalent bond. Used to break polymers into monomers.
  - *Reactants:* polymer + water → *Product:* monomers → *Side product:* none
  - Example: digestion of **starch** into glucose.
- **Enzymes** are needed for cells to carry out these reactions. An **enzyme** speeds up a chemical reaction; it is **not consumed** and **not changed** — enzymes are **catalysts**.

## 2.3 Carbohydrates

- **Functions:** energy source; provide building material (structural role).
- Contain **carbon, hydrogen and oxygen in a 1 : 2 : 1 ratio**.
- Three varieties: **monosaccharides, disaccharides, polysaccharides**.

### Monosaccharides
- A **monosaccharide** is a single sugar molecule (a **simple sugar**).
- Backbone of **3 to 7 carbon atoms**.
- **Hexoses** (six carbons): **glucose** (blood sugar), **fructose** (fruit sugar), **galactose**. Formula **C₆H₁₂O₆**.
- **Pentoses** (five carbons): **ribose** and **deoxyribose** (the sugars in nucleotides, the monomer of DNA).

### Disaccharides
- A **disaccharide** = two monosaccharides joined by **dehydration synthesis**.
- **Lactose** (milk sugar) = galactose + glucose
- **Sucrose** (table sugar) = glucose + fructose
- **Maltose** = glucose + glucose  →  forms **maltose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁)** + water
- **Lactose-intolerant** people lack the enzyme **lactase**, which breaks lactose into galactose and glucose.

### Polysaccharides — energy-storage and structural molecules
A **polysaccharide** is a polymer of monosaccharides.

| Polysaccharide | Role |
| -------------- | ---- |
| **Starch** | Energy storage in **plants** (amylose = unbranched; amylopectin = branched) |
| **Glycogen** | Energy storage in **animals** (highly branched; stored in liver) |
| **Cellulose** | Makes up **plant cell walls** (glucose chains held by hydrogen bonds → microfibrils) |
| **Chitin** | Cell walls of **fungi** and exoskeleton of some animals |
| **Peptidoglycan** | Cell walls of **bacteria** |

## 2.4 Lipids

- **Varied** in structure; large, **nonpolar** molecules that are **insoluble in water**.
- **Functions:** long-term energy storage · structural components · heat retention · cell communication and regulation · protection.
- Varieties: **fats, oils, phospholipids, steroids, waxes**.

| Type | Function | Human uses |
| ---- | -------- | ---------- |
| Fats | Long-term energy storage and insulation in animals | Butter, lard |
| Oils | Long-term energy storage in plants and seeds | Cooking oils |
| Phospholipids | Component of plasma membrane | Food additive |
| Steroids | Component of plasma membrane (cholesterol), sex hormones | Medicines |
| Waxes | Protection, prevention of water loss (plant cuticle), beeswax, earwax | Candles, polishes |

### 1. Triglycerides — long-term energy storage
- Also called **fats** and **oils**.
- Consist of **one glycerol** molecule linked to **three fatty acids** by dehydration synthesis.
- Fatty acids may be **saturated** or **unsaturated**:
  - **Saturated** — **no** double bonds between carbons; tend to be **solid** at room temperature (e.g. butter, lard).
  - **Unsaturated** — **one or more** double bonds; tend to be **liquid** at room temperature (e.g. plant oils). Groups can be on the same side (**cis**) or opposite sides (**trans**) of the double bond.
  - **Trans fat** — a triglyceride with at least one bond in the **trans** configuration; made when unsaturated fat is converted to saturated fat by **hydrogenation**, which changes the molecule's shape.

### 2. Phospholipids — membrane components
- Structure similar to triglycerides, but consist of **one glycerol + two fatty acids + a modified phosphate group**.
- The **fatty acid tails** are **nonpolar** and **hydrophobic**.
- The **phosphate head** is **polar** and **hydrophilic**.
- **Function:** form the **plasma membranes** of cells.
- In water, phospholipids form a **lipid bilayer** (double layer):
  - Polar phosphate **heads** face the **water**.
  - Nonpolar fatty acid **tails** face **away** from water, forming a **hydrophobic core**.
  - **Kinks** in the tails keep the membrane **fluid** across a range of temperatures.

![Phospholipid structures and the lipid bilayer in water](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Phospholipids_aqueous_solution_structures.svg?width=520)
*Image: "Phospholipids aqueous solution structures" by LadyofHats, Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

### 3. Steroids — four fused carbon rings
- Composed of **four fused carbon rings** with various functional groups attached.
- **Functions:** component of the **animal cell membrane**, regulation.
- Examples: **cholesterol, testosterone, estrogen**.
- Testosterone and estrogen are **sex hormones** that differ **only** in the functional groups attached to the same carbon skeleton.
- **Cholesterol** is the **precursor** for several other steroids, but can also contribute to **circulatory disorders**.

### 4. Waxes
- Long-chain fatty acids connected to carbon chains containing **alcohol** functional groups.
- **Solid** at room temperature, **waterproof**, **resistant to degradation**.
- **Function:** protection.
- Examples: **earwax** (contains cerumen), **plant cuticle**, **beeswax**.

## 2.5 Proteins

- Proteins are polymers of **amino acids** linked by **peptide bonds**.
  - A **peptide bond** is a **covalent bond** between amino acids.
  - As much as **50 %** of the dry weight of most cells is protein.
- Two or more amino acids joined = **peptide**; long chains = **polypeptide**.
- A **protein** is a polypeptide that has **folded into a particular shape**, which is essential for it to work.

### Functions of proteins
1. **Metabolism** — most **enzymes** are proteins that catalyse reactions in cells.
2. **Support** — structural proteins such as **keratin** and **collagen**.
3. **Transport** — membrane channel and carrier proteins control what enters/exits cells; **hemoglobin** carries oxygen.
4. **Defense** — **antibodies** bind antigens and stop them destroying cells.
5. **Regulation** — **hormones** are regulatory proteins that influence cell metabolism.
6. **Motion** — **microtubules** move cell parts; **actin and myosin** let muscles contract.

### Amino acids — the monomers
- There are **20** different common amino acids.
- Each has an **amino group (H₂N–)**, an **acidic group (–COOH)**, and a **variable R group**.
- Amino acids differ by their **R group**, which may be **nonpolar (hydrophobic)**, **polar (hydrophilic)**, or **ionized**.
- **Peptide synthesis:** amino acid + amino acid → **dipeptide + water** (dehydration). The reverse is **hydrolysis**.

### Denaturation
- A protein must fold into its proper shape to function.
- When a protein **loses its proper shape**, it is **denatured**.
- Caused by certain **chemicals**, a change in **pH**, or **high temperature**.

### The 4 levels of protein structure
1. **Primary** — the **linear sequence** of amino acids (coded in the genes of DNA). Changing the sequence produces a different protein.
2. **Secondary** — local folding into **alpha helices** and **beta (pleated) sheets**, held by **hydrogen bonds**.
3. **Tertiary** — the overall **3-D shape** of a polypeptide; stabilized by **hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen, ionic, and covalent (disulfide) bonds**.
4. **Quaternary** — **more than one** polypeptide joined together.

## 2.6 Nucleic Acids

- Nucleic acids are polymers of **nucleotides**. Two varieties:
  - **DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)** — genetic material that stores information for its **own replication** and for the **sequence of amino acids in proteins**.
  - **RNA (ribonucleic acid)** — performs many functions, including **protein synthesis** and **regulation of gene expression**.

### Structure of a nucleotide
Each nucleotide has **three parts**:
- a **phosphate group**
- a **pentose sugar**
- a **nitrogen-containing (nitrogenous) base**

There are **five** types of nucleotides:
- **DNA** contains **adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine**.
- **RNA** contains **adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil**.
- **Purines:** adenine, guanine. **Pyrimidines:** cytosine, thymine (DNA), uracil (RNA).

### Structure of DNA and RNA
- Nucleotides join by **dehydration synthesis** into a **strand** (a sequence of nucleotides).
- The **backbone** is made of alternating **sugar–phosphate** molecules.
- **RNA** is mostly **single-stranded**; **DNA** is **double-stranded**.
- DNA's two strands are held together by **hydrogen bonds** between the bases and twist into a **double helix**.
- **Complementary base pairing:**
  - **Adenine (purine) pairs with Thymine (pyrimidine).**
  - **Cytosine (pyrimidine) pairs with Guanine (purine).**
  - So the number of **A + G (purines)** always equals the number of **T + C (pyrimidines)**.

| | DNA | RNA |
| - | --- | --- |
| **Sugar** | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| **Bases** | Adenine, guanine, thymine, cytosine | Adenine, guanine, uracil, cytosine |
| **Strands** | Double-stranded with base pairing | Single-stranded |
| **Shape** | Helix | Linear |

![Comparison of DNA and RNA structure](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Difference_DNA_RNA-EN.svg?width=520)
*Image: "Difference DNA RNA-EN" by Sponk, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

### ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate)
- **ATP** is a nucleotide made of **adenine + ribose (= adenosine)** and **three phosphates**.
- It is a **high-energy** molecule because of the **last two unstable phosphate bonds**, which are easily broken.
- **Hydrolysis** of the terminal phosphate bond yields:
  - **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate)
  - an **inorganic phosphate (P)**
  - **energy** to do cellular work

## Summary

| Biomolecule | Monomer / subunit | Polymer / large molecule |
| ----------- | ----------------- | ------------------------ |
| Carbohydrates | Monosaccharides | Polysaccharides |
| Lipids | Glycerol + fatty acids | Triglyceride |
| Proteins | Amino acids | Polypeptide |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotides | DNA, RNA |

- **Synthesis = Dehydration:** monomers → polymer (+ water).
- **Degradation = Hydrolysis:** polymer + water → monomers.
