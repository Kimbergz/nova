# Topic 4: Biology of the Plasma Membrane

**Outline:** 4.1 Structure & Function · 4.2 Passive Transport · 4.3 Active Transport · 4.4 Modification of Cell Surfaces

> The plasma membrane is a thin, flexible boundary that **surrounds every cell**. It is only **7–10 nanometres** thick, yet acts as a **highly selective barrier** controlling everything that enters and leaves the cell. If this balance is lost, the cell can lose function or die within minutes.

---

## 4.1 Plasma Membrane: Structure and Function

### The Fluid Mosaic Model
The plasma membrane is made of a **phospholipid bilayer** with proteins and other molecules embedded in it.
- **Fluid** — lipids and proteins can move **laterally** within the membrane.
- **Mosaic** — many different types of molecule are embedded in the bilayer.

![The fluid mosaic model of the plasma membrane](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Cell_membrane_detailed_diagram_en.svg?width=600)
*Image: "Cell membrane detailed diagram" by Mariana Ruiz (LadyofHats), Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

**Key functions:**
- **Separation & protection** — separates the cell from its external environment.
- **Transport & communication** — membrane proteins transport materials, transmit information, and act as enzymes.
- **Cell adhesion** — cell-adhesion molecules connect cells to form tissues.
- **Homeostasis** — regulates what enters and leaves, keeping a stable internal environment.

### The phospholipid bilayer
Phospholipids are **amphipathic** (have both a water-loving and a water-avoiding part):
- **Hydrophilic (polar) head** — a negatively charged phosphate group; interacts with water.
- **Hydrophobic (non-polar) tails** — two fatty acid chains; repel water.

Because they are amphipathic, phospholipids spontaneously form a **bilayer** in water (heads face the watery outside and inside; tails point inward, away from water). The membrane is **flexible** (changes shape without breaking), **self-sealing** (rounds up into closed vesicles), and can **fuse** with other bilayers.

**Movement of phospholipids:** lateral movement (sideways) is **frequent**; transverse **flip-flop** across the membrane is **rare**, because the hydrophilic head would have to cross the hydrophobic core (energetically unfavourable).

### Other lipids & cholesterol — affect membrane fluidity
- In cold conditions, some organisms increase the proportion of **unsaturated fatty acids** in their membrane lipids.
- **Unsaturated** tails have **kinks** at their carbon–carbon double bonds that **hinder close packing** → more **fluid** membrane.
- **Saturated** tails pack closely → more **viscous** membrane.

### Membrane proteins
Proteins sit in different positions on or within the bilayer. **Two types:**
- **Integral membrane proteins** — amphipathic proteins firmly bound to the membrane. **Transmembrane proteins** are integral proteins that extend completely through the membrane.
- **Peripheral membrane proteins** — on the inner or outer surface, bound to exposed regions of integral proteins.
- (**Lipid-anchored proteins** are covalently attached to a lipid in one leaflet and do not span the membrane.)

**Six functions of membrane proteins:**
1. **Channel protein** — allow passage of molecules/ions through a channel. *e.g. aquaporin, calcium channel.*
2. **Carrier protein** — combine with the substance and assist its passage (undergo a conformational change). *e.g. sodium–potassium pump.*
3. **Cell recognition protein** — glycoproteins/glycolipids on the extracellular surface (ID tags); carbohydrate chains of fewer than 15 sugars; help the body recognise foreign substances. *e.g. MHC (major histocompatibility complex) glycoproteins.*
4. **Receptor protein** — bind specific signal molecules, letting the cell respond to signals from other cells. *e.g. hormone receptor protein.*
5. **Enzymatic protein** — some are enzymes that carry out metabolic reactions directly. *e.g. ATP synthase complex.*
6. **Junction protein** — form junctions and attach adjacent cells. *e.g. desmosome (animal), plasmodesmata (plant).*

### Permeability of the plasma membrane
The membrane is **selectively permeable** — it lets some substances through but not others.
- **Small, non-charged molecules** (CO₂, O₂, glycerol, alcohol) pass **freely**, following the concentration gradient.
- **Water** crosses through a special protein called **aquaporin**, which speeds up water transport.
- **Charged molecules and ions** (and larger polar molecules) are **inhibited** from passing.

## 4.2 Passive Transport

Substances cross the membrane in two main ways: **passively** (no energy) or **actively** (energy required).

**Passive transport** = movement of molecules **down** a concentration gradient (high → low); facilitated by channel and carrier proteins; **does NOT require ATP**. Examples: simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis.

### Diffusion
Movement of molecules **down a concentration gradient**. The rate of diffusion depends on the particles' **size and shape**, their **electric charge**, and the **temperature**. Two types:
1. **Simple diffusion** — small non-polar molecules (O₂, CO₂) move directly through the bilayer.
2. **Facilitated diffusion** — polar molecules and ions move through **channel or carrier proteins** (e.g. glucose, amino acids, Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻). Transport proteins are **specific** and undergo a **conformational change** to move the molecule; still **no ATP** used.

### Osmosis
- A special case of diffusion focusing on **solvent (water)** movement rather than solute.
- The diffusion of **water** across a selectively permeable membrane.
- Net movement of water is toward the side with **lower water (higher solute)** concentration.
- **Osmotic pressure** is the pressure that develops due to osmosis.

### Tonicity — the effect of osmosis on cells
**Tonicity** = the relative concentrations of solutes in two fluids separated by a selectively permeable membrane. It tells us whether a cell will **swell, shrink, or stay the same**.

![Effect of tonicity (isotonic, hypotonic, hypertonic) on red blood cells](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Osmotic_pressure_on_blood_cells_diagram.svg?width=560)
*Image: "Osmotic pressure on blood cells diagram" by Mariana Ruiz (LadyofHats), Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

| Solution | Meaning | Animal cell | Plant cell |
| -------- | ------- | ----------- | ---------- |
| **Isotonic** | Solute equal on both sides; no net water movement | Normal shape | **Flaccid** (normal) |
| **Hypotonic** | Solute **lower** outside → water enters, cell **swells** | May **lyse** (burst) | **Turgid** (firm) from turgor pressure |
| **Hypertonic** | Solute **higher** outside → water leaves, cell **shrinks** | **Crenation** (shrivels) | **Plasmolysis** (cytoplasm pulls from wall) |

**Real-life examples:**
- **Hypotonic:** drinking excess pure water can swell/lyse red blood cells; medical IV fluids (e.g. 0.45 % saline) gently hydrate; freshwater protozoans use **contractile vacuoles** to pump out excess water.
- **Hypertonic:** salting/curing preserves food by dehydrating bacteria; drinking sea water dehydrates you (salt pulls water out); soy sauce causes extreme crenation of RBCs; salt gargle draws fluid from swollen throat tissue.
- **Isotonic:** IV fluids like 0.9 % saline must match blood's tonicity to prevent cell damage.

## 4.3 Active Transport

**Active transport** = movement of molecules or ions **against** their concentration gradient (low → high) using **energy (ATP)**, facilitated by specific carrier proteins (pumps). It is essential for maintaining cellular **homeostasis** and gradients.

### 1. Sodium–Potassium Pump
- Uses **ATP** to pump **Na⁺ out** of the cell and **K⁺ into** the cell, against their gradients.
- For every **3 Na⁺** pumped out, **2 K⁺** are pumped in.
- This creates **membrane polarization**, a **membrane potential**, and **electrochemical gradients** that store energy to drive other transport systems.

### Bulk transport (via vesicles)
**2. Exocytosis — release out of the cell**
- Intracellular vesicles **fuse** with the plasma membrane to **release** their contents outside.
- Used to secrete hormones, neurotransmitters, digestive enzymes, and to remove waste.
- The vesicle membrane **becomes part of** the plasma membrane (both are nonpolar). Vesicles are produced by the **Golgi body**.
- Steps: vesicle moves to membrane → docks and fuses → contents released outside → vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.

**3. Endocytosis — bringing into the cell**
The cell membrane engulfs substances into a pouch that becomes a **vesicle** inside the cell. Three ways:
- **Phagocytosis** ("cell eating") — large, solid material taken in (e.g. human white blood cells engulfing debris or viruses).
- **Pinocytosis** ("cell drinking") — vesicles form around liquid or very small particles.
- **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** — a specific form of pinocytosis using **receptor proteins** and a **coated pit** to selectively take in substances.
- General steps: substance binds membrane/receptor → membrane invaginates into a pouch → pouch pinches off into a vesicle → vesicle moves in → a **lysosome** fuses and releases digestive enzymes.

![The three types of endocytosis: phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Endocytosis_types.svg?width=560)
*Image: "Endocytosis types" by Mariana Ruiz (LadyofHats), Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

> **Passive vs Active:** passive transport needs **no energy** and moves substances **down** the gradient; active transport **requires energy** to move substances **against** the gradient.

## 4.4 Modification of Cell Surfaces

Cell surfaces can be modified by the **extracellular matrix (ECM)** — a meshwork of **proteins** and **polysaccharides** in close connection with the cells that produced them.

**Components of the ECM:**
1. **Collagen** — provides tensile strength and structural support.
2. **Elastin** — provides elasticity; lets tissues stretch and recoil.
3. **Fibronectin** — a glycoprotein that helps cells adhere to the ECM and to each other.
4. **Integrin** — transmembrane receptor proteins connecting the ECM to the cytoskeleton inside the cell.
5. **Proteoglycans** — a core protein with attached carbohydrate chains; provide hydration and resistance to compression.

### Cell junctions (animal cells)
Specialized connections between adjacent cells that let them behave in a coordinated manner.

| Feature | Gap junction | Tight junction | Adhesion junction (desmosome) |
| ------- | ------------ | -------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Function** | Communication between cells | Barrier/seal to prevent leakage | Strong adhesion between cells |
| **Structure** | Protein channels (**connexons**) link cytoplasm | Membranes tightly pressed with sealing proteins | Dense plaques and filament proteins (**cadherins**) |
| **Allows passage of** | Ions, small molecules (e.g. glucose, ions) | Nothing (forms a seal) | No exchange; mechanical strength |
| **Found in** | Heart muscle, smooth muscle, some epithelia | Intestinal lining, kidney tubules, blood vessels | Skin, uterus, heart (high-stress areas) |

### Plant cell surface modifications
- **Plasmodesmata** — cytoplasmic protein channels through the cell walls; allow **cell-to-cell communication and transport** (water, ions, small solutes, RNA, proteins). Found in **plant cells only**; passive for small molecules, regulated for large/polar molecules.
- **Cuticle** — a waxy, non-living layer covering the epidermis; made of **cutin**; reduces water loss and protects against pathogens.
- **Cell wall** — rigid layer outside the plasma membrane; made mainly of **cellulose**, hemicellulose and pectin; provides shape, support and protection.
- **Middle lamella** — a thin, non-cellulosic layer between adjacent cell walls; made mainly of **pectin**; acts as a "cement" holding cells together.

---

## Summary

- **Structure:** phospholipid bilayer + membrane proteins (integral & peripheral) + cholesterol → the **fluid mosaic model**.
- **Selective permeability:** small non-polar molecules pass directly; polar molecules and ions need transport proteins.
- **Passive transport** (no ATP, down gradient): diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion.
- **Active transport** (ATP, against gradient): sodium–potassium pump, endocytosis, exocytosis.
- **Cell-surface modifications:** ECM and cell junctions (animals); cell wall, cuticle, plasmodesmata, middle lamella (plants).
