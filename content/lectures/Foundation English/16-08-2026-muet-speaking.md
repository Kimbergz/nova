# MUET Speaking (Paper 800/2)

> **Note on accuracy:** exact mark allocations per criterion are set internally by MPM and not always published in full detail on public sources. The structure, timing, and general marking *areas* below are consistent across MPM specimen materials and tuition-centre resources, but treat exact percentages with caution and confirm against the current official syllabus where precision matters (e.g. right before your exam).

## 1. The format at a glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Duration | 30 minutes total |
| Total marks | 90 (25% of final grade) |
| Number of parts | 2 |
| Group size | Usually 2–4 candidates per session |

### Part 1 — Individual Presentation

- You are given a **written prompt/stimulus** (a picture, statement, or short scenario).
- **2 minutes** to prepare, silently, using notes if allowed.
- **2 minutes** to speak, alone, in front of the examiner(s) and your fellow candidates.
- No interruptions from others during this part — it is entirely your own performance.

### Part 2 — Group Discussion

- The group is given **one task with 5 written prompts/points** to discuss (e.g. "Discuss the following ways to reduce plastic waste in schools").
- **3 minutes** of individual silent preparation — you privately decide your stance and which point(s) you want to speak on.
- **8–12 minutes** of open discussion among the group, with no further prompting from the examiner. You must manage turn-taking yourselves.
- The group is expected to reach some form of conclusion or consensus by the end, though examiners are mainly marking *how* you get there, not just the final decision.

## 2. What the examiners are actually listening for

Across MPM speaking assessment, candidates are broadly judged on four areas:

1. **Task fulfilment / content** — did you actually address the topic, with relevant, developed ideas (not just a list of one-word points)?
2. **Language** — range and accuracy of vocabulary and grammar; can you express nuance, not just simple sentences?
3. **Communicative ability** — fluency, coherence, appropriate turn-taking, and (in Part 2) how well you interact: inviting others in, responding to what's said, not just delivering a rehearsed speech.
4. **Pronunciation / intelligibility** — can a listener understand you without excessive strain, even if your accent isn't "native"?

**Key insight:** a candidate who speaks fluent, grammatically perfect English but never responds to what teammates say, or dominates without listening, is marked down on communicative ability. MUET Speaking is not a monologue contest — especially in Part 2.

## 3. Part 1 technique: the individual presentation

With only 2 minutes to prepare and 2 minutes to speak, structure beats improvisation. A reliable skeleton:

1. **Opening (5–10 seconds):** Restate the topic in your own words — don't just read the prompt aloud. *"The picture shows..."* / *"I'd like to talk about..."*
2. **2–3 main points**, each with a brief example or reason. Do not try to cram in five ideas — a well-developed two points beats five underdeveloped ones.
3. **Closing (5–10 seconds):** A short concluding sentence so your talk doesn't just trail off. *"In conclusion, I believe..."* / *"Overall, this shows that..."*

**Preparation-time tip:** jot down 3–4 keywords per point, not full sentences. If you write full sentences you'll be tempted to read them robotically, which hurts your communicative-ability and pronunciation scores (flat, unnatural delivery).

**Time-management tip:** 2 minutes is roughly 150–200 spoken words at a natural pace. Practise with a stopwatch so you learn what "too short" and "too long" feel like in your body, not just on paper.

## 4. Part 2 technique: the group discussion

### How to open strongly
Being the first to speak is a genuine advantage — it shows initiative and confidence, and sets the tone. A simple, natural opener:

> *"Shall we start? I think all five points are important, but I'd like to begin with [point], because..."*

### Useful language toolkit

| Function | Useful phrases |
|---|---|
| Starting | "Shall we begin?" / "I'd like to start by saying…" |
| Giving an opinion | "In my opinion…" / "I feel strongly that…" / "From my point of view…" |
| Agreeing | "I completely agree with you because…" / "That's a good point, and I'd add that…" |
| Disagreeing politely | "I see your point, but I think…" / "I'm not sure I fully agree, because…" |
| Inviting others in | "What do you think, [name]?" / "Do you have anything to add?" |
| Interrupting politely | "Sorry, may I just add something here?" / "If I could just come in here…" |
| Summarising / concluding | "So, to sum up, we agree that…" / "It seems we all think that…" |

### Turn-taking discipline
- Speak roughly your fair share — with 3–4 people and 8–12 minutes, aim for several shorter turns rather than one long monologue at the start and silence after.
- Actively bring in a quieter group member — this single move is one of the most reliable ways to boost your communicative-ability score, because it demonstrates genuine interaction rather than parallel monologues.
- Respond to what was just said before pivoting to your own point — "Building on what Aisyah said about cost, I'd add that…" shows you were listening, not just waiting for your turn.

## 5. Worked example

**Task:** *Discuss the following ways schools could encourage students to read more:*
1. Weekly reading hour
2. School library upgrades
3. Reading rewards/incentive schemes
4. Peer book clubs
5. Author visits

**Sample opening turn:**
> *"I think all these ideas are useful, but I'd like to start with point 3 — reading rewards. In my school, we had a system where students got small prizes for finishing a certain number of books, and honestly, it worked — even students who never used to read started borrowing books just to get the reward. What do you all think about that?"*

Notice this turn: restates the point chosen, gives a personal/concrete example (not abstract opinion), and ends by explicitly inviting the next speaker — exactly the combination examiners reward.

## 6. Common mistakes that cost marks

- Memorising and reciting a "template speech" that doesn't actually respond to the specific prompt — examiners notice generic, prompt-independent answers immediately.
- In Part 2, one or two candidates dominating while others barely speak — this hurts *everyone's* communicative-ability marks, including the dominant speaker's.
- Speaking too quietly or looking only at notes instead of the group/examiner — pronunciation and intelligibility marks depend on being heard and understood.
- Disagreeing bluntly ("No, you're wrong") instead of politely — MUET rewards the *language of negotiation*, not just having a strong opinion.
- Running out of things to say because only one-word ideas were prepared — always prepare a reason and an example for each point, not just the point itself.
- Ending the discussion abruptly with no summary when time is nearly up — a brief "so, to conclude…" shows awareness and control.

## 7. How to practise effectively

- Practise Part 1 with a real stopwatch and record yourself — listen back for filler words ("um," "like"), pace, and whether you actually finished your conclusion.
- Practise Part 2 with 3 friends using **real 5-point prompts** (past-year or specimen topics), rotating who "starts" so you get comfortable being first, middle, and last speaker.
- Build a personal phrase bank (from the table above) and actively use different phrases each session — relying on the same 2–3 phrases every time sounds mechanical to examiners who hear hundreds of candidates.
- Watch or listen to real debates/discussions in English to internalise natural turn-taking rhythm, not just vocabulary.

## 8. Quick pre-exam checklist

- Comfortable structuring a 2-minute talk with opening–points–closing in under 2 minutes of prep.
- Have a working phrase bank for agreeing, disagreeing, and inviting others in.
- Practised actively including quieter group members, not just delivering your own points.
- Rehearsed a natural (not robotic) speaking pace using notes-as-keywords, not full scripts.
- Comfortable politely disagreeing and summarising a discussion under time pressure.
