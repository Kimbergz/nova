# MUET Writing (Paper 800/4)

> **Note on accuracy:** as with the other papers, MPM occasionally revises minor details (exact word counts, prompt lengths). The structure below reflects current specimen materials, but confirm exact minimum word counts on your actual exam paper's instructions — they are always printed there.

## 1. The format at a glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Duration | 75 minutes total |
| Total marks | 90 (25% of final grade) |
| Number of tasks | 2 |
| Suggested time split | ~25 min for Task 1, ~50 min for Task 2 |

### Task 1 — Email/Letter Response
- You're given a prompt of roughly **100–135 words** (often an email or a description of a situation with several points you must respond to).
- You must write a response of **at least 100 words**.
- Usually addresses a personal, semi-formal, or formal situation — replying to a friend, a school, or an organisation.

### Task 2 — Essay
- You're given a shorter **40–80 word** scenario, statement, or problem to respond to.
- You must write an essay of **at least 250 words**.
- Common essay types: **discursive** (weighing different views), **argumentative** (arguing one clear position), and **problem–solution** (identifying a problem and proposing workable solutions).

## 2. What the examiners are actually marking

Across both tasks, MUET writing is generally assessed on three broad areas:

1. **Content / task fulfilment** — did you address *every* point in the prompt, with ideas that are relevant and adequately developed (not just a bare mention)?
2. **Organisation / coherence** — is there a clear structure (intro–body–conclusion for essays; logical paragraphing for the email), with ideas linked smoothly using discourse markers?
3. **Language** — range and accuracy of grammar and vocabulary; can you express ideas precisely, and are errors minor/non-obstructive rather than frequent and meaning-obscuring?

**Key insight for both tasks:** missing even one sub-point in the prompt caps your content score, no matter how beautiful your English is elsewhere. Always number the points in the prompt and physically tick each one off as you write.

## 3. Task 1 technique: email/letter

### Structure
1. **Greeting** — match the tone of the situation: *"Hi [Name],"* for a friend, *"Dear Sir/Madam,"* or *"Dear Mr./Ms. [Name],"* for a formal recipient.
2. **Opening line** — acknowledge what prompted your reply. *"Thank you for your email about…"* / *"I was glad to hear that…"*
3. **Body (2–4 short paragraphs)** — address each point raised in the prompt, one idea per paragraph where possible. Use discourse markers to connect ideas: *firstly, in addition, however, as for, regarding.*
4. **Closing line** — a natural wrap-up matching the tone. *"I hope this helps!"* / *"I look forward to hearing from you."*
5. **Sign-off** — *"Best regards,"* / *"Your friend,"* + name (or a placeholder name if none given).

### Model language bank

| Function | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Acknowledging | "Thank you for letting me know…" / "I was pleased/sorry to hear…" |
| Explaining | "The reason is…" / "This is mainly because…" |
| Adding a point | "In addition,…" / "On top of that,…" |
| Contrasting | "However,…" / "On the other hand,…" |
| Requesting | "I was wondering if…" / "Would it be possible to…" |
| Closing | "Please let me know if you need anything else." / "I hope to hear from you soon." |

### Worked mini-example
**Prompt (paraphrased):** *A friend emails asking what you enjoyed most at the school Science Fair, whether you joined any competitions, and whether you'd go again next year.*

**Response opening:**
> *Hi Aiman,*
> *Thanks for asking about the Science Fair — it was honestly one of the best events I've been to this year! My favourite part was the interactive exhibits, especially the slime-making booth, where we got to mix the chemicals ourselves.*
> *As for competitions, I actually joined the robot-building challenge with two classmates. We didn't win, but we learned a lot about problem-solving under time pressure.*
> *I'd definitely go again next year — maybe we could join together this time?*
> *Best,*
> *[Your name]*

Notice every question from the prompt is answered in order, in its own mini-paragraph, with natural (not robotic) transitions.

## 4. Task 2 technique: the essay

### Universal structure
1. **Introduction (2–4 sentences):** a brief hook (a fact, a rhetorical question, or a general statement about the topic), followed by a clear **thesis statement** — one sentence stating your overall position or the essay's focus.
2. **Body paragraphs (2–3):** **one main idea per paragraph.** Each paragraph should follow point → explanation → example/evidence. Never blend two unrelated ideas into one paragraph.
3. **Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** restate your thesis in different words, briefly summarise your main points, and (for problem-solution essays especially) end with a forward-looking statement or call to action.

### By essay type

**Discursive** (weighing multiple viewpoints):
- Dedicate roughly one paragraph to each side, then a final paragraph (or your conclusion) giving your own considered view.
- Useful language: *"Proponents argue that… On the other hand, critics point out that… Taking both sides into account, I am of the opinion that…"*

**Argumentative** (arguing one position):
- State your position clearly in the introduction and don't hedge — the whole essay should build the case for it.
- Useful language: *"It is evident that…" / "This clearly demonstrates that…" / "For these reasons, I firmly believe that…"*

**Problem–solution**:
- Paragraph 1 (after intro): explain the problem and why it matters.
- Paragraph 2–3: propose solutions, briefly weighing practicality, cost, and feasibility for each.
- Useful language: *"One effective solution would be to…" / "This would help by…" / "A more practical alternative might be…"*

### Worked mini-example (problem–solution, opening only)
**Prompt (paraphrased):** *Many teenagers today spend excessive time on social media. Discuss the problem and suggest solutions.*

> *In recent years, the amount of time teenagers spend scrolling through social media has risen dramatically, raising concerns among parents and educators alike. While social media offers genuine benefits for communication and learning, excessive use can harm academic performance, sleep, and mental well-being. This essay will examine the causes of this problem and propose several practical solutions.*

This opening does three things efficiently: sets context, acknowledges nuance (avoids sounding one-dimensional), and previews the essay's direction — exactly what a strong thesis paragraph should do.

## 5. Common mistakes that cost marks

- Writing under the minimum word count (100 for Task 1, 250 for Task 2) — this directly caps your content/task-fulfilment score.
- Copying phrases directly from the prompt instead of paraphrasing — examiners are trained to notice, and it signals limited independent language range.
- Ignoring one of the points/questions in the Task 1 prompt entirely.
- Cramming multiple unrelated ideas into a single body paragraph instead of one clear idea per paragraph.
- Overusing memorised "big words" or idioms incorrectly in an attempt to sound advanced — inaccurate sophisticated language scores worse than accurate simple language.
- Weak or missing conclusions — trailing off without restating the thesis or summarising leaves organisation marks on the table.
- Spending so long on Task 2 that Task 1 is rushed (or vice versa) — stick to your time budget.

## 6. How to practise effectively

- Practise both tasks **under strict time limits** (25 min / 50 min) so word-count and structure become automatic, not something you have to think about mid-exam.
- Build a personal bank of discourse markers and topic-specific vocabulary (environment, education, technology, health are recurring MUET essay themes) — but practise using them naturally in full sentences, not as a checklist to insert randomly.
- After each practice essay, self-mark against the three criteria (content, organisation, language) rather than just checking grammar — this trains you to think like the examiner.
- Get a teacher, tutor, or peer to review your writing periodically; self-editing alone tends to miss recurring personal error patterns (e.g. consistent tense mistakes or article errors).

## 7. Quick pre-exam checklist

- Know both tasks' minimum word counts and suggested time budget cold.
- Have a reliable, memorised paragraph structure for email/letter and for each essay type (discursive, argumentative, problem-solution).
- Comfortable paraphrasing prompt language instead of copying it.
- Practised ticking off every point in the Task 1 prompt before finishing.
- Built and rehearsed a personal bank of linking phrases and topic vocabulary.
- Practised writing a strong thesis statement and a matching conclusion under time pressure.
