## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture, students should be able to recognize postharvest handling practices for three types of produce:

- Crop yields (fruits and vegetables)
- Livestock yields (meat and poultry)
- Fishery products

**Specific objectives:**

- Understand product maturation and maturity indices.
- Understand the postharvest handling activities.
- Understand a few pre-cooling methods.
- Identify the preparation of vegetables for fresh market.
- Identify the preparation of fruits for fresh markets.

## What Is Postharvest Management?

Postharvest management is the set of methods and techniques applied **to increase the shelf life of the products**. It covers everything that happens to a crop after it leaves the field: harvesting, handling, storage, processing, packaging, transportation, and marketing.

The postharvest system should deliver yields (crops, livestock, fishery) from the time and place of harvest to the time and place of consumption, with **minimum loss, maximum efficiency, and maximum return** for everyone involved — the farmer, the trader, and the consumer.

Fruits, vegetables, meat, and fish are among the most perishable agricultural produce. A key principle runs through this whole lecture: **quality cannot be improved after harvest — it can only be maintained.** Good production practices in the field are what set the ceiling for how good (and how long-lasting) the final product can be.

## 1. Postharvest Handling for Crops (Fruits & Vegetables)

### 1.0 Harvesting

Harvesting is the first — and one of the most decisive — steps in the postharvest chain.

- Carry it out carefully **to minimize mechanical injury** such as scratches, punctures, and bruises.
- Harvest during the **coolest time of day** (early morning) — produce isn't exposed to the heat of the sun, and harvesters work more efficiently.
- If harvesting in the hotter part of the day can't be avoided, **keep the produce shaded in the field** to reduce weight loss and wilting.
- **Harvesting at the proper maturity** stage also extends shelf life.

![Orange harvest](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Orange_Harvest.jpg?width=800)

### 1.1 Handling

The skin of a fruit or vegetable is its main defence: it's an effective barrier against most of the opportunistic bacteria and fungi that cause rotting. Breaking that skin also speeds up physiological deterioration and dehydration. This is why all horticultural produce should be handled gently, minimizing bruising and breaks in that soft outer layer.

**Dumping** is the first activity within handling — moving produce out of the field container into the packing line — and it should always be done gently:

| Method | How it works | Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| **Wet dumping** | Produce is immersed in water | Reduces mechanical injury, bruising, and abrasions |
| **Dry dumping** | Soft brushes fitted on a sloped ramp or moving conveyor belt | Removes dust and dirt from the fruit surface |

### 1.2 Pre-Cooling

Once harvested, vegetables and fruits keep respiring — taking in oxygen and giving off carbon dioxide. Reducing that respiration rate is the single most effective way to prolong shelf life and maintain quality, and the simplest way to reduce respiration is to **cool the product quickly**. Pre-cooling also limits moisture loss in the crop.

Four common pre-cooling methods:

**(A) Room cooling** — Low-cost, slow method. Produce is simply loaded into a cool room and cool air is allowed to circulate among the cartons, sacks, bins, or pallet bins.

**(B) Forced-air cooling** — The fastest pre-cooling method and the one most widely used across horticultural produce. Cold air is pulled or pushed through vents/holes in the storage containers; uniform cooling is achieved when pallet-bin stacks are properly aligned.

**(C) Hydro-cooling** — One of the fastest precooling methods. Produce is cooled rapidly by direct contact with cold, moving water. Its advantage is that it doesn't remove water from the produce, and can even revive slightly wilted produce.

**(D) Vacuum cooling** — Cooling happens through water evaporation from the product at very low air pressure, inside a sealed steel chamber from which air is pumped out. It causes roughly **1% produce weight loss (mostly water) for every 6 °C of cooling**.

![Cold storage room](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Cold_Room.jpg?width=800)

### 1.3 Washing and Cleaning

Washing controls the build-up of disease-causing inoculum during pack-house operations.

- Example: washing with a chlorine solution at **100–150 ppm**.
- Best pH for the wash solution: **6.5–7.5**.
- Also used to remove latex from produce such as mangoes and bananas.

### 1.4 Sorting & Grading

- Sorting separates poor produce from good produce.
- Systematic sorting extends shelf life, maintains wholesomeness and freshness, keeps quality consistent, and substantially cuts losses.
- Crops destined for storage should be as free as possible from skin breaks, bruises, spots, rot, decay, and other deterioration.
- Sorting also limits the spread of infection to other, still-healthy fruits and vegetables.

### 1.5 Packaging

- Proper packing is essential to maintain the freshness of leafy vegetables.
- Packaging should be designed to prevent premature deterioration in product quality.
- Use **clean, smooth, and ventilated** containers — an important factor in cutting losses during transport, marketing, and storage.
- Cushioning materials (dry grass, paddy straw, leaves, sawdust, paper shreds) help dissipate the heat generated by the produce's own respiration.
- Common container types: pallet boxes, fibre-board boxes, plastic boxes, wooden crates, and woven baskets — chosen according to the produce and the distribution channel.

### 1.6 Storage

- Only crops of high initial quality can be stored successfully — storage cannot rescue produce that was already damaged or over-mature.
- Shelf life is extended by keeping a commodity at its optimal temperature, relative humidity, and environmental conditions.
- Most fruit and vegetable crops keep better quality at **high relative humidity (80–95%)**.

## Product Maturation and Maturity Indices

Every fruit or vegetable passes through three stages in its life span:

1. **Maturation** — Completed natural growth and development; the produce is ready for harvest.
2. **Ripening** — Follows or overlaps maturation, rendering the produce edible, as shown by taste.
3. **Senescence** — The final stage, marked by natural degradation of the fruit or vegetable, such as loss of texture and flavour.

### How maturity is determined

| Indicator | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Skin colour** | Changes as fruit ripens/matures — seen in rambutan, apple, tomato, papaya |
| **Shape** | Bananas become more rounded in cross-section; mango shoulders may rise above the point of attachment |
| **Size** | E.g. the width of individual banana fingers, measured with calipers |
| **Aroma** | Some fruit develop a characteristic smell as they ripen |
| **Leaf changes** | In potatoes, harvest time is soon after the leaves and stems have died |
| **Firmness** | Measured with a pressure tester — texture changes as the fruit matures |
| **Sugars** | Some fruit accumulate starch during maturation, which later breaks down into sugar as the fruit ripens; others accumulate sugar directly |
| **Days from full bloom** | Elapsed time between flowering and harvest |
| **Abscission layer** | Development of a natural separation layer at the fruit stalk (clearly visible in durian, for example) |

![A bunch of bananas](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/A_bunch_of_bananas.jpg?width=700)

A firmness (pressure) tester is a simple, practical tool: a probe is pushed into the fruit and a gauge reads the force needed to penetrate it. As tomatoes ripen from green to red, for instance, their measured firmness drops sharply — roughly 272 kPa when green down to about 63 kPa when fully ripe.

## 2. Postharvest Handling for Livestock & Poultry

### Key definitions

- **Abattoir** — A food factory where every operation is governed by standards of hygiene and animal welfare, aiming to produce wholesome meat for consumers in the most humane way possible.
- **Carcass** — The harvested, dressed animal, with the hide, hooves, head, and internal organs removed.
- **Lairage** — The place where animals are rested before slaughter, to preserve enough glycogen for it to be converted into lactic acid.

### Slaughtering

Slaughtering refers to the killing of domestic livestock. It involves initial cutting to open the major body cavities and remove the entrails, usually keeping the carcass in one piece, which is then butchered into smaller cuts. It can be carried out by two broad methods:

| Traditional | Modern |
|---|---|
| **Halal** — throat cut in a single stroke | **Pre-slaughter stunning** — captive bolt pistol, electrical stunning, or gas (CO₂) stunning |
| **Kosher** — a swift cut severs major neck blood vessels | **Bleeding** — major blood vessels are cut |
| **Jhatka** — the animal is killed by a single stroke severing the head | **Restraint & handling**, followed by **mechanical/automated slaughter** |

### Pre-slaughter factors affecting the process

Environmental factors (temperature, humidity), improper resting time, feeding, stress during loading/unloading, and general "strangeness" can all affect meat quality. These stresses lead to two well-known quality defects:

- **DFD (Dark, Firm, Dry)**
- **PSE (Pale, Soft, Exudative)**

### Slaughtering process of poultry (step by step)

1. **Procurement** — the age of the bird is judged by feeling the pliability of the posterior breast with the fingers.
2. **Lairage** — birds rest for about 6 hours to preserve glycogen reserves.
3. **Ante-mortem inspection** — carcasses are checked for obvious signs of disease, extreme emaciation, and lumps.
4. **Bleeding** — done for 2 minutes per bird; roughly 60% of the blood is lost on sticking, 22–25% remains under the viscera, and 10% stays in the carcass.
5. **Scalding** — birds are dipped in hot water to loosen feather follicles (dip-scald or spray-scald).
6. **Picking** — feather removal by hand-picking, a blunt knife, or mechanical pickers.
7. **Pinning** — removal of remaining pin-like feathers with a knife.
8. **Singeing** — remaining filoplumes are burned off with a flame for surface sterilization and a clean, uniform appearance.
9. **Evisceration / cropping and venting** — removal of viscera, crop, neck, and any faecal matter in the lower intestine; the carcass is then washed immediately.
10. **Postmortem inspection** — the carcass is checked externally for disease, bone abnormalities, wounds, and tumours; the liver and spleen are checked for consistency, texture, and colour changes.
11. **Washing** — the dressed carcass is washed again with a clean water spray, ideally at 10–15 °C.
12. **Chilling and draining** — carcasses are chilled to an internal temperature of 4 °C for 30–35 minutes, then drained on racks for 10 minutes to remove excess water.
13. **Grading** — carcasses are classified by conformation, fat covering, defeathering, exposed flesh, discolouration, disjointed/broken bones, missing parts, freezing defects, bruising, and other quality attributes.
14. **Packaging** — extends shelf time; examples include vacuum packaging and controlled-atmosphere packaging.
15. **Storage** — dressed chicken can be kept refrigerated at 2 °C for up to 7 days, or deep-frozen at −18 °C to −20 °C for 4–6 months.

## 3. Postharvest Handling for Fish

Fish is one of the most perishable of all food commodities, so speed and cold temperature dominate every step of the chain:

1. **Harvest / capture** — fish are removed from cages or ponds using nets, preferably in the early morning or late evening to reduce heat stress.
2. **Bleeding, gutting, and deheading** — fish are bled and gut/viscera removed soon after harvest.
3. **Washing and cleaning** — fish are washed with potable/clean water to remove blood, slime, and debris.
4. **Chilling and icing** — fish are placed on ice immediately to lower their surface temperature.
5. **Sorting, grading, and inspection** — sorted by size, species, and condition; inspected for defects, with damaged fish discarded.
6. **Packing and packaging** — packed into insulated, clean containers such as ice boxes or insulated crates.
7. **Storage** — chilled product is kept near melting ice (about 0–4 °C); frozen product is kept at ≤ −18 °C, avoiding temperature fluctuations.
8. **Transport to market and retail** — using refrigerated trucks or insulated vehicles, replenishing ice as needed.
9. **Retail display and sale** — chilled fish is displayed on ice or in cold display cabinets.

![Fish on ice](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Ice_fresh_anchovy.jpg?width=800)

### Postharvest losses of fish

Fish is a highly perishable commodity, so it is prone to high postharvest losses — both **physical (material)** losses and **quality** losses. **Post-harvest fish loss (PHFL)** refers to fish that is either discarded or sold at a relatively low price because of quality deterioration. This costs fishers, processors, and traders potential income, and it also means consumers end up with less fish available, or fish of lower quality.

Very high levels of loss tend to occur during pre-processing, processing, storage, and transportation. Common causes include discarding fish that is too small or not valuable enough to land, poor processing techniques, animal predation and insect infestation, inadequate packaging/storage, and — above all — high ambient temperature, the single biggest driver of fish deterioration.

**Where losses occur along the chain:**

| Stage | Example causes | Type of loss |
|---|---|---|
| During fishing | Destructive/harmful fishing methods; fish falling from the net or discarded as bycatch; fishing gear left too long | Physical, Quality |
| Holding fish on board | Delayed return to landing, exposing fish to heat at sea; failure to gut/wash/chill on board; stepping on fish | Quality, Physical |
| During unloading | Poor hygienic practices; fish falling from pan/crate/basket; theft during offloading | Quality, Physical |
| Fresh fish marketing | Inadequate icing and no insulated container; lack of market information / oversupply; deliberate delay in purchasing by traders | Quality, Physical, Market |
| Processing & packaging | Processing already spoiled fish; unhygienic conditions inviting blowfly infestation; poor packaging; oxidation of fatty fish causing rancidity | Physical, Quality |
| During storage | Mould growth and dampness; insects; discolouration from chemical changes; inadequate facilities; transport damage | Physical, Quality |
| During marketing | Delays in selling; inadequate cold storage/warehouses and lack of ice; wrong-time supply; poor buyer purchasing power | Quality, Physical, Market |

## Key Takeaways

- Quality is locked in at harvest — postharvest handling can only *preserve* it, never improve it.
- Gentle handling, prompt cooling, and clean water/sanitation are the common threads across crops, poultry, and fish.
- Maintaining the right temperature and humidity throughout storage and transport (the "cold chain") is the single biggest lever for extending shelf life and cutting losses in every commodity covered in this lecture.
- Losses can creep in at almost every stage of the chain — from harvest to retail — so postharvest management has to be treated as a continuous system, not a single step.
