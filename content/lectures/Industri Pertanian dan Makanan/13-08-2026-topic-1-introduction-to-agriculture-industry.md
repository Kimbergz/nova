# Topic 1: Introduction and Importance of Agriculture Industry to the Country

**Outline:** 1.1 What is Agriculture? · 1.2 Development of Agriculture · 1.3 Farming Practices · 1.4 Major Sectors in Agricultural Industry · 1.5 Agricultural Agencies in Malaysia · 1.6 National Agro-food Policy & Self-Sufficiency Levels · 1.7 Importance of Agriculture to the Country

> Agriculture touches almost everything in daily life — the rice on your plate, the rubber in your car tyres, the wood in your pencil, even the mint in your toothpaste. This topic looks at how agriculture developed, how Malaysia organises its agricultural industry, and why the sector still matters so much to the country's economy and food security.

## 1.1 What is Agriculture?

**Agriculture** comes from two Latin words:
- **Agri / Ager** — field
- **Cultura** — cultivation / tillage of the soil

**Definition:** the science or art of cultivating the soil, or the culture of plants or animals in water systems, and the associated activities needed to **produce, harvest and market** useful crops and/or **raise animals for profit** *(Lewis, R. A., 2002, CRC Dictionary of Agricultural Sciences)*.

## 1.2 Development of Agriculture

Agriculture has moved through a long **transition from low-tech to high-tech mechanization**:

| Period | Technological achievement |
| ------ | -------------------------- |
| Before 1900s | **Manual** labour (human and animal power) |
| 1950–1970 | **Tractors and machines** |
| 1990 | **Computers and information technology** |
| 1994 | **Satellites and sensors** |
| 2000 | **Mobiles and database software** |
| Present | **IoT, big data, and Artificial Intelligence (AI)** |

**Low-tech vs high-tech mechanization:**

| | Low tech | High tech |
| - | -------- | --------- |
| **Role** | Replaces/supports only the mechanical functions of humans | Supports or **replaces human functionality and capability** |
| **Flexibility** | Limited to simple tasks | **Flexible** across many tasks |
| **Human role** | A human operator is still in charge of supervision and safety | Runs on **software-based AI**, needing less direct human control |
| **Technology base** | Little electronics or software | Mix of mechanics and electronics (**mechatronics/robotics**) |
| **Examples** | Tractors, ploughs, seeders, planters, harvesters | Autonomous tractors, autonomous harvesting |

## 1.3 Farming Practices

Agricultural practices are broadly grouped into **subsistence farming** and **commercialised farming**.

### Subsistence farming
Food is produced **mainly for the farmer and their family's own consumption**; mostly found in less-developed countries.

- **Intensive subsistence agriculture** — the most abundant type of agriculture practised worldwide; highly **labour-intensive**; practised on limited space with limited waste; common in **East Asia, South Asia and Southeast Asia**, where population density is high and land is limited.
- **Shifting cultivation** — farmers move to new locations every few years to farm fresh land; a common method from the **first agricultural revolution**. Two steps:
  1. **Slash-and-burn** — clearing land by cutting and burning vegetation.
  2. Growing crops on the cleared land for **2–3 years** until the soil's nutrients are depleted, then moving on.

![Slash-and-burn shifting cultivation in Thailand](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Slash_and_Burn_Agriculture_Shifting_Cultivation_Thailand.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Slash and Burn Agriculture, Shifting Cultivation, Thailand" by mattmangum, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0).*

### Commercialised farming
The primary objective is to **make a profit** — food is produced for sale in the global marketplace (**agribusiness**), rarely sold directly to the consumer; usually sold to a food-processing company.

**Key features:**
- **Monoculture**, or a combination of only a few crops.
- **High-yielding, modern varieties or clones.**
- **Large chemical inputs** — pesticides, fertilizers.
- **Modern facilities, technology and machines** used for planting, maintenance and harvesting.

**Types of commercialised farming:** plantation crops, aquaculture, livestock farming, horticulture, food crops.

## 1.4 Major Sectors in Agricultural Industry

Malaysia's agricultural industry is generally divided into three major sectors: **Crops, Livestock, and Fisheries.**

### Crops
Crop components are divided into groups according to **usage**:

| Group | Examples |
| ----- | -------- |
| **Food crops** | Paddy, maize, sago, tapioca, sweet potatoes; **in temperate regions**: wheat, barley, oats, rye, potatoes |
| **Industrial crops** (also known as **plantation crops**) | Oil palm, rubber tree, cocoa, coconut, etc. |
| **Horticultural crops** | Fruits, vegetables, flowers, ornamentals, turf grass, etc. |
| **Oil crops** | Peanut, maize, rapeseed, sunflower, oil palm, coconut, olive, jatropha, etc. |

![Oil palm plantation in Malaysia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Oilpalm_malaysia.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Oilpalm Malaysia" by Craig (Pizzaboy1), Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

### Livestock
**Livestock** are domesticated animals reared for food, normally grouped into three categories:

| Category | Features | Examples |
| -------- | -------- | -------- |
| **Mono-gastric** | Stomach/digestive system has **one compartment** | Swine, horse |
| **Ruminants** | **4-compartment stomach** (rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum); efficient converters of grass/forage into meat | Goat, sheep, cattle, buffaloes |
| **Poultry** | Have **feathers** and **lay eggs** | Chicken, ducks, quails, ostrich, goose, swan, peacock |

### Fisheries
The **fisheries subsector** splits into a food fish sector and a non-food fish sector:
- **Food fish sector** — marine capture fisheries, inland fisheries, aquaculture fisheries.
- **Non-food fish sector** — ornamental fish.

![Floating aquaculture cage off the coast of Penang, Malaysia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Southern_Penang_and_the_floating_aquaculture_cage_%282538298949%29.jpg?width=600)

## 1.5 Agricultural Agencies in Malaysia

To achieve the objectives of the National Agriculture Policy, the government set up **ministries, departments and agencies** to hasten agricultural development and to help farmers, smallholders, fishermen and livestock breeders.

### Three key ministries and their agencies

| Ministry | Agencies under it |
| -------- | ------------------ |
| **Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security (MAFS)** | Department of Agriculture Malaysia (DOA) · Department of Fisheries Malaysia (DOFM) · Department of Veterinary Services (DVS) · Federal Agricultural Marketing Authority (FAMA) · Malaysian Agricultural Research and Development Institute (MARDI) |
| **Ministry of Plantation and Commodities** | Malaysian Cocoa Board (MCB) · Malaysian Palm Oil Board (MPOB) · Malaysian Rubber Board (MRB) |
| **Ministry of Rural and Regional Development** | FELCRA Berhad · Rubber Industry Smallholders Development Authority (RISDA) |

### Farmers' associations
Give farmers **bargaining power** for better produce prices and for the wellbeing of their members. Examples:
- Persatuan Peladang (district and state levels)
- Persatuan Pekebun-pekebun Kecil Malaysia
- Persatuan Penternak (cow, goat, chicken)
- Dairy Farmers Association
- Persatuan Nelayan
- Koperasi Nelayan

### Corporate and estate agencies (private sector)
Many corporate and estate agencies are involved in cultivating and managing **oil palm, rubber, cocoa, fruit, coconut**, and related processing, manufacturing and export industries. Major players include:
- **Sime Darby** (merged with Guthrie and Golden Hope)
- **IOI**
- **FELDA and FGV**
- **Lembaga Tabung Haji**
- **Lembaga Tabung Angkatan Tentera (LTAT)**
- **Borneo Samudera & SLDB** (Sabah)
- Others: Dunlop, Socfin, KL-Kepong, etc.

Together, **universities, government agencies and private sectors** form the network of agricultural agencies and potential employers of agriculture graduates.

## 1.6 National Agro-food Policy & Self-Sufficiency Levels

### National Agro-food Policy 2021–2030 (DAN 2.0 / NAP 2.0)
Enacted to **drive economic growth**, **improve people's well-being**, and **prioritise food security and nutrition**. It focuses on **two main aspects**:
- **Food security** — ensuring food supplies are available so everyone always has access to sufficient, nutritious food.
- **Economic growth** — the Agrofood sector's contribution to **GDP**, and raising the income of food producers.

It focuses on **four sub-sectors**: paddy and rice-based products, fruits and vegetables, livestock, and fisheries and aquaculture.

### Agriculture sector in Malaysia — current status
- The sector shows **positive growth**, reflecting the effect of national master-plan strategies.
- Production of most agro-food commodities increased only **marginally** due to **low productivity**.
- **Production growth is smaller than demand growth**, so Malaysia still **relies on imports** of agro-food commodities from global markets.
- The agricultural sector's **value-added contribution to GDP has shrunk**, alongside a transition toward other economic activities.

### Self-Sufficiency Level (SSL) of agro-food commodities

**Self-Sufficient Level (SSL)** shows how much of a commodity's domestic demand is met by domestic production (100% = fully self-sufficient).

| Sector/Item | 2010 (%) | 2020ᶠ (%) | CAGR 2010–2020ᶠ (%) |
| ----------- | -------- | --------- | -------------------- |
| Rice | 62.95 | 63.00 | 0.01 |
| Fruits | 83.73 | 79.50 | (0.52) |
| Vegetables | 49.61 | 51.50 | 0.37 |
| Beef | 30.12 | 21.72 | (3.22) |
| Mutton | 11.89 | 10.72 | (1.03) |
| Pork | 95.25 | 91.62 | (0.39) |
| Poultry Meat | 105.55 | 104.51 | (0.10) |
| Poultry Egg | 114.63 | 116.60 | 0.17 |
| Milk | 99.60 | 62.40 | (4.57) |
| Fisheries | 94.89 | 93.51 | (0.15) |

*ᶠ = forecast; figures in brackets are negative CAGR.*

**Key takeaways:**
- **Rice, vegetables, and chicken/duck eggs** saw an **increase** in SSL.
- **Other major agro-food commodities** saw a **reduction** in SSL.
- **Poultry meat and poultry eggs** stayed **above 100% SSL** from 2010–2020 (Malaysia is a net exporter of these).
- **Fresh milk's SSL dropped sharply — by 37.35%** — from 2010 to 2020, mainly due to **rising consumption**.

## 1.7 Importance of Agriculture to the Country

### 1. Support livelihood
- Agriculture helps **reduce poverty**, raise incomes and improve **food security** for the **80% of the world's poor** who live in rural areas and work mainly in farming.
- It sustains village economies by generating **food, employment**, and other livelihoods.
- It is a **major employer in rural areas** — for daily-wage labourers as well as skilled workers.

### 2. Providing raw materials
Many raw materials come from agriculture — from **timber** for construction to **herbs** for flavouring food.
- **Corn** — used for food and as the base for **ethanol** biofuel.
- **Resins** — plant products used in adhesives, coatings and paints.

### 3. Encouraging economic development
Agriculture is tied to other sectors of the economy, so it **impacts global trade**, supports **job creation**, and drives **economic development**. Countries with strong agricultural sectors tend to see:
- Employment growth in other sectors.
- Higher per-capita incomes.
- Technology innovation and improved farm management to boost productivity and profitability.

### Products derived from agriculture
Agriculture meets consumer and business demand across an interconnected world economy, supplying: **fruits and vegetables, animal feed, natural rubber, cotton for clothing, biofuels, industrial products, and pharmaceutical products.**

![Fresh local produce for sale at Kea Farm, Cameron Highlands, Malaysia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Kea_Farm_produce.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Kea Farm produce" by AyyanD, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).*

### Agriculture in everyday life
Agricultural products are woven into daily routines:

| Activity | How agriculture is involved |
| -------- | ---------------------------- |
| **Shelter** | Wood and bamboo for décor and construction |
| **Morning routine** | Mint flavouring in toothpaste; caffeine from coffee beans |
| **Dressing up** | Cotton, hemp, ramie, flax for clothing; bio-based skin creams and shampoos |
| **Cleaning** | Aloe vera and citrus used in cleaning products |
| **Education** | Wood-based pencils, paper textbooks |
| **Driving to work** | Natural rubber (rubber trees) for tyres; leather car seats from livestock |
| **Entertainment** | Paper from trees; wooden musical instruments (reed, ukulele, violin, guitar) |

## Summary

- **Agriculture** = cultivating soil, plants and/or animals to produce, harvest and market crops or raise animals for profit.
- **Development of agriculture**: manual labour (pre-1900s) → tractors/machines (1950s–70s) → computers/IT (1990) → satellites/sensors (1994) → mobiles/database software (2000) → IoT, big data, AI (present).
- **Farming practices**: **subsistence** (intensive subsistence, shifting cultivation — for the farmer's own use) vs **commercialised** (agribusiness, monoculture, high inputs — for profit).
- **Major sectors**: **crops** (food, industrial, horticultural, oil crops), **livestock** (mono-gastric, ruminants, poultry), and **fisheries** (food fish and non-food fish sectors).
- **Agencies**: three ministries (MAFS, Plantation and Commodities, Rural and Regional Development) each oversee specialised agencies; plus farmers' associations and private corporate/estate agencies (Sime Darby, IOI, FELDA/FGV, etc.).
- **NAP 2.0 (DAN 2.0), 2021–2030**: focuses on food security and economic growth across paddy/rice, fruits & vegetables, livestock, and fisheries & aquaculture.
- **SSL data (2010–2020)**: rice, vegetables and eggs improved; beef, mutton, milk and fruits declined; poultry stayed above 100%; milk fell sharply (37.35%).
- **Importance of agriculture**: supports rural livelihoods, supplies raw materials, drives economic development, and underpins countless everyday products — from food and clothing to fuel, medicine and entertainment.
