# Topic 2: Crop Production

**Outline:** 2.1 Cropping Patterns · 2.2 Cropping Systems · 2.3 Other Agricultural Practices · 2.4 Growing Media · 2.5 Fertilisers · 2.6 Pest & Disease Management

> Before a single seed is sown, a farmer must decide **how** the crops will be laid out in the field, **which system** they will be grown under, and **what inputs** — soil, food, and protection — will keep them alive until harvest. This topic walks through all three decisions, from the geometry of an orchard to the chemistry of a fertiliser bag.

---

## 2.1 Cropping Patterns

**Cropping pattern** = the arrangement of crops in a particular land area.

**Why does the arrangement matter?** A good cropping pattern is designed to:
- Use orchard space and other resources **efficiently**.
- Maximise **solar radiation interception** and distribution within the canopy → best fruit quality and yield.
- **Minimise competition** between trees for nutrients and moisture through proper spacing.
- Stay **compatible** with management practices — pruning, thinning, harvesting, pest control, etc.

**The four common layout patterns** (mainly used for orchard/plantation trees):

| Pattern | Layout | Notes |
| ------- | ------ | ----- |
| **Square** | Plants at right angles; every 4 plants form a square; row-to-row and plant-to-plant spacing equal | Most common and easiest to lay out; interculture (inter-row work) possible in both directions |
| **Triangular** | Same as square, but alternate rows (2nd, 4th, 6th...) are shifted midway between the rows before them | Gives more open space for trees and for intercropping |
| **Hexagonal** | Trees at the corners of equilateral triangles; six trees form a hexagon around one central tree | Fits **~15% more trees** than the square pattern, but harder to lay out and cultivate |
| **Rectangular** | Plot divided into rectangles (not squares); trees at the four corners, rows at right angles | Like the square system, allows interculture in two directions |

*Example:* for oil palm planted in a triangular pattern, tightening the spacing from 10.0 m to 8.5 m raises planting density from about 115 to 160 palms per hectare — spacing choice directly controls how many productive trees fit on the land.

## 2.2 Cropping Systems

Crops are planted and managed under one, or a combination, of **four systems**: mono-cropping, mixed cropping, rotational/relay cropping, and cash cropping.

| System | Malay term | Core idea |
| ------ | ---------- | --------- |
| **Mono-cropping** | *penanaman tunggal* | A single crop species is planted, managed, harvested, then re-planted |
| **Mixed cropping** | *penanaman campuran* | Two or more species are planted together in the same area/season |
| **Rotational (relay) cropping** | *penanaman giliran* | Different species are planted in **succession**, one per season, on the same land |
| **Cash cropping** | *penanaman kontan* | A fast-yielding crop is planted **between the rows** of a slow-yielding main crop |

### Mono-cropping
A single species is managed alone until harvest, then the land is re-planted with the same crop.
- **Short-term (annual) crops** — paddy, maize, peanut, soybean, tapioca are grown as a sole crop on a moderate scale and harvested after **3–6 months**, then re-planted with the same species the next season.
- **Plantation (perennial) crops** — rubber, oil palm, cocoa, coffee are planted on a large commercial scale (often thousands of hectares) as a sole crop.
- Perennial mono-crops are kept for **25–30 years** or until their economic yield declines, then felled (zero burning) and replanted — often with newly-developed clones or varieties.
- Major plantation companies (e.g. IOI, Sime Darby, Sawit Kinabalu, SLDB, FELDA, Sabah Tea) favour mono-cropping for **easier management, operation, and processing**.

### Mixed cropping
Two or more crop species — sometimes with different maturation periods (perennial + annual together) — are planted in the same area at the same time, arranged in **alternate rows** or a **haphazard/orchard style**.

**Examples from the deck:**
- Maize planted with hill/dry-land paddy.
- Peanut planted in the inter-rows of a tapioca crop.
- Different fruit species + spices + vegetables grown together.
- Coconut + wet paddy in low-lying areas.

![Maize and beans grown together as an intercrop](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Intercropping_maize_and_beans.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Intercropping maize and beans" by AnnaJB, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).*

### Rotational (relay) cropping
One species is planted in a season; a **different** species follows on the same land the next season. Practised by smallholders, farmers, and research stations in both tropical and temperate countries — usually with short-term crops.

**A typical 4-cycle sequence:**
1. **Shallow-rooted crop** — maize
2. **Legume** — groundnut (enriches the soil)
3. **Vegetable crop** — okra/chilies
4. **Deep-rooted crop** — tapioca, then the cycle repeats

- A **legume** is included because its nutrient-rich vegetative parts are chopped and ploughed in as **green manure**.
- Crops in the sequence are chosen to have **different rooting depths**.

**Advantages of crop rotation:**
- Reduces outbreaks of **pests and diseases**.
- **Weed control** — dense-foliage crops (e.g. potato, squash) suppress weeds, easing the load on the crops that follow.
- Nutrients from **different soil depths** are absorbed by successive crops with different root systems.

**Rotation in wet paddy fields:** paddy is grown in the rainy (main) season where irrigation is unavailable; in the off-season (dry paddy field), tobacco, roselle, or vegetables (long bean, sawi, chilies) are planted instead — or cattle are allowed to graze the field if nothing is planted.

![Small-scale crop rotation plots in a vegetable garden](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Crop-rotation.JPG?width=600)
*Image: "Crop rotation" by Sten Porse, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

### Cash cropping
A short-term crop (annual/biennial) is planted **between the rows** of a perennial main crop (e.g. oil palm, rubber) while that main crop is still too young to yield income (up to ~3 years). The cash crop yields within **3–5 months**, giving farmers ready cash. It is only planted during the main crop's **first 2–3 years** — after that, the canopy shades the inter-rows and the cash crop can no longer produce viable yields.

**Why it matters:**
- Gives smallholders quick income while waiting for the main crop.
- Generates export income for the country.
- Helps offset vegetable prices.
- Common cash crops: chili, cucumber, ginger, maize, pineapple, groundnut, banana, long bean.

**Common main crops:** oil palm, rubber, coconut, cocoa, and fruit species with a long juvenile period.

## 2.3 Other Agricultural Practices

Beyond the four core cropping systems, three broader practices are also used:

**Integrated farming** — combines crop species, livestock/poultry, and fish rearing (aquaculture) in one sustainable system. Commonly includes crop production, livestock, freshwater aquaculture (often for hobby fishing), on-site processing facilities, mushroom cultivation, and agro-tourism/family-day facilities. In an *ecological farming system*, crops, livestock, and fish ponds are linked — feed and manure cycle between them, and a **biodigester** processes effluent, closing the loop.

**Organic farming** — relies on non-chemical techniques such as crop rotation, green manure, compost, and biological pest control.

| Benefit | Why it works |
| ------- | ------------- |
| **Reduces chemical exposure** | Avoids pesticide resistance build-up in pests/weeds/pathogens; prevents pesticides contaminating soil, water, and air (residues can persist for decades) |
| **Combats soil erosion** | Decomposing organic matter binds soil together; raising organic matter from 1% to 3% can cut erosion by 20–33% by boosting water-holding capacity |
| **Discourages algal blooms** | Blooms are driven by chemical-fertiliser runoff; biological fertilisers (green manure, compost) reduce this risk |
| **Encourages biodiversity** | A rich mix of microorganisms, plants, and animals builds healthy soil and resilient crops without needing chemical intervention |

**Agro-forestry** — industrial crops, food crops, fruit trees, landscape plants, and medicinal plants are inter-planted with forest species (e.g. sentang, jati, kapur baji, urat mata beludu) as the main crop; livestock are sometimes integrated too.
- **Fruit trees:** salak, durian, mangosteen, jackfruit, cempedak, dokong.
- **Landscape plants:** palms, ixora, heliconia, wild ginger, bougainvillea.
- **Livestock:** goats and sheep (free grazing), or dairy/beef cattle in a feed-lot system.
- **Freshwater fish:** tilapia, Japanese carp, grass carp.
- Examples: coffee bushes grown with forest species in Malaysia; durian-based agroforestry; forest species planted within rice fields in Indonesia.

## 2.4 Growing Media

**Growing medium** = the substance through which plant roots grow and extract water and nutrients. Native plant nurseries may use native soil, but growers more often use an **"artificial soil"** made of materials such as peat moss or compost.

| Medium | What it is | Key property |
| ------ | ---------- | ------------- |
| **Cocopeat** | Derived from coconut husks | Improves soil drainage |
| **Perlite** | Expanded volcanic glass, heated to 1000 °C until it "pops" (like popcorn) | Extremely porous — absorbs water well |
| **Peat moss** | Layers of partially decomposed moss/grass from bogs and swamps; takes centuries to form | Rich in organic matter, holds moisture |
| **Sand** | Naturally occurring granular material, varies in texture/colour | Promotes air flow in soil |

## 2.5 Fertilisers

**Fertiliser** = any organic or inorganic material, natural or synthetic, added to soil to supply one or more essential plant nutrients.

Fertilisers are broadly **organic** or **chemical**, and come in three physical forms: **granules, powder, and liquid**. Granular fertilisers are further divided by nutrient content:
- **Straight** — supplies a single nutrient (e.g. urea, rock phosphate, ammonia, ammonium sulfate, muriate of potash).
- **Mixture** — a blend of more than one straight fertiliser, supplying multiple nutrients.
- **Compound** — every granule contains multiple nutrients, giving uniform micronutrient distribution through the root zone; more expensive than the other types.

### Applying solid fertiliser
| Method | How it's done |
| ------ | -------------- |
| **Broadcasting — Top dressing** | Fertiliser spread over closely-sown standing crops; must avoid direct contact with the plant (burning/scorching) and heavy rain (leaching/wash-off) |
| **Broadcasting — Basal application** | Fertiliser spread evenly over the soil and mixed in, before or during sowing |
| **Placement — Plough sole** | Fertiliser laid in a continuous band at the bottom of the plough furrow, covered as the next furrow is turned; suits soils that dry out a few inches below the surface |
| **Placement — Deep placement** | A fertiliser pellet/briquette is placed near the root zone (centred between 4 plants, 7–10 cm deep, within 7 days of transplanting), by hand or machine; releases nitrogen gradually, reducing N losses and improving uptake efficiency |

Placement (rather than broadcasting) is generally used when the fertiliser quantity is small or the crop's root system is still poorly developed.

![Tractor broadcasting granular fertiliser over a field](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/A_Dusting_of_Fertilizer_near_Mayall%27s_Coppice_-_geograph.org.uk_-_156926.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "A Dusting of Fertilizer near Mayall's Coppice" by Bob Embleton, Wikimedia Commons/Geograph (CC BY-SA 2.0).*

### Applying liquid fertiliser
- **Foliar application** — a small amount of fertiliser/mineral is sprayed directly onto the leaves.
- **Injection into soil** — liquid or gaseous fertiliser is placed below the soil, near the roots.
- **Fertigation** — water-soluble fertilisers/chemicals are distributed through the irrigation system.
- **Aerial application** — crops are sprayed with crop-protection products from an agricultural aircraft.

### Why crops need fertiliser — nutrient deficiency
A **nutrient deficiency** occurs when a plant lacks enough of an essential nutrient for growth.
- **Nitrogen (N)** — builds plant protein, encourages healthy foliage growth.
- **Phosphorus (P)** — helps grow strong roots.
- **Potassium (K)** — supports proper growth and reproduction; affects plant shape, fruit size, colour, and taste.

## 2.6 Pest & Disease Management

**Pesticide** = any substance or mixture of substances intended for preventing, destroying, repelling, or mitigating a pest. Every pesticide contains an **active ingredient**, an **inert ingredient**, and possible **contaminants/impurities**.

Pesticides may be **organic** or **chemical**, and are grouped by target: **fungicides, insecticides, herbicides, bactericides, rodenticides**.

**Pesticide hazard classification:**

| Class | Hazard statement | Band colour |
| ----- | ----------------- | ----------- |
| Ia | Very toxic | Black |
| Ib | Toxic | Red |
| II | Harmful | Yellow |
| III | Caution | Blue |
| IV | (least hazardous) | White |

### Plant disease & pest management — four approaches
1. **Exclusion** — keep pathogens, vectors, and infected plants out of disease-free areas altogether (e.g. never plant diseased or infested stock).
2. **Eradication** — eliminate, destroy, or inactivate a disease organism once it is established: destroy infected plants, disinfect storage bins/containers/equipment, and use cultural practices such as crop rotation and weeding.
3. **Protection** — build a chemical or physical barrier between the host and the disease-causing agent: fungicides, insecticides, herbicides; row covers, fences, and other physical barriers.
4. **Resistance** — plant resistant varieties, where the host's genetic make-up has been altered to be less susceptible to the disease organism.

![Aerial application of pesticide over a field](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Cropduster_spraying_pesticides.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Cropduster spraying pesticides" by Charles O'Rear, USDA, Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

**Common plant diseases:**

| Disease | Symptoms | Cause |
| ------- | -------- | ----- |
| **Black spot** | Black, round spots on the upper side of leaves (roses and other ornamentals); lower leaves infected first; severe cases turn yellow and drop | Fungal |
| **Powdery mildew** | White, powdery substance on upper leaf surfaces (also stems, flower buds, fruit) | Fungal |
| **Canker** | Sunken, swollen, cracked, or dead areas on stems, limbs, or trunk (mainly woody plants) | Fungal or bacterial |

## Summary

- **Cropping pattern** = the geometric arrangement of crops in a field (square, triangular, hexagonal, rectangular), chosen to balance space use, sunlight capture, and ease of management.
- **Cropping systems:** **mono-cropping** (one species, replanted after harvest), **mixed cropping** (several species together), **rotational cropping** (different species in succession — improves soil, controls pests/weeds), and **cash cropping** (a fast crop between rows of a slow main crop for quick income).
- **Other practices:** integrated farming (crops + livestock + fish), organic farming (no synthetic chemicals — reduces exposure, erosion, and algal blooms while boosting biodiversity), and agro-forestry (crops inter-planted with forest species).
- **Growing media** (cocopeat, perlite, peat moss, sand) support root growth and water/nutrient uptake, especially where natural soil is unsuitable.
- **Fertilisers** (organic/chemical; granule, powder, or liquid) supply nutrients such as N, P, and K; applied by broadcasting, placement, or liquid methods (foliar, injection, fertigation, aerial).
- **Pest and disease management** combines exclusion, eradication, protection, and resistant varieties, guided by a pesticide hazard classification system (Class Ia–IV).
