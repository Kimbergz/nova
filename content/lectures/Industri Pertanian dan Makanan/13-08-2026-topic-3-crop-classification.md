# Topic 3: Crop Classification

**Outline:** 3.1 Three Approaches to Classification · 3.2 Classification by Life Cycle · 3.3 Classification by Use / Commodity · 3.4 Horticulture Crops in Detail · 3.5 Botanical & Morphological Classification

> There are more than **300,000 plant species** on Earth. To make sense of them, agriculturists sort crops using different criteria depending on the purpose. A single crop can belong to several systems at once — **durian**, for example, is a *perennial* crop, a *fruit (horticultural) crop*, and botanically a *dicotyledon*, all at the same time.

---

## 3.1 Three Approaches to Classification

Crops are commonly classified in three main ways:

| Classification system | Basis | Main categories | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **By life cycle** | Duration from planting/germination to completion of the vegetative & reproductive stages | Annual, Biennial, Perennial | Maize, paddy (annual) · carrot, cabbage (biennial) · oil palm, durian, rubber (perennial) |
| **By use/commodity** | Primary economic use or product | Food, Plantation/industrial, Fibre & forage, Horticulture (fruit, vegetable, ornamental) | Rice (food) · oil palm, rubber (industrial) · kenaf (fibre) · durian, orchid (horticulture) |
| **By botanical & morphological traits** | Taxonomic rank & physical structure (leaf, flower, fruit, seed shape) | Monocot vs Dicot; Order–Family–Genus–Species–Cultivar | Maize, palms, bamboo (monocot) · durian, rubber (dicot) |

A crop is **not limited to one category** — classification depends on the purpose. Durian, for instance, is *perennial* by life cycle, a *fruit crop* by use, and falls under *pomology* in horticulture, all at once.

---

## 3.2 Classification by Life Cycle

### i) Annual crops
Complete their entire growth and production cycle **within one year or less**. They grow, mature, and produce harvestable products (seeds, fruits, leaves, roots or tubers) in the same year, and must usually be **replanted from seed or vegetative material** for the next cycle.

![Paddy field, an example of an annual food crop](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Sekinchan%20Paddy%20Field.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Sekinchan Paddy Field" by Auyong83, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).*

| Crop | Life cycle length |
| --- | --- |
| Paddy | 125–135 days (variety-dependent) |
| Maize | 90–120 days (60–75 days for sweet corn) |
| Peanut | 120–150 days |
| Cassava | 8–10 months (6–8 months for sweet type) |
| Vegetables | 4–5 weeks (salad, water spinach) to 2–3 months (chilli, tomato, eggplant) |

*Other examples:* water spinach, chilli pepper.

### ii) Biennial crops
Complete their life cycle **within two years**.
- **Year 1** — mainly vegetative growth; food reserves stored in roots, stems or leaves (the harvest stage for root crops).
- **Year 2** — reproductive growth: flowers, fruits and seeds, after which the plant must be **re-planted** for another cycle.

*Examples:* **carrot** (*Daucus carota*) — storage root in year 1, flowering/seed in year 2; **cabbage** (*Brassica oleracea* var. *capitata*) — compact head in year 1, flowers/seeds in year 2.

### iii) Perennial crops
Live for **more than two years** and keep producing harvestable output for many years **without needing replanting after each harvest**. Plantation crops are usually only replanted once productivity declines with age (e.g. oil palm is replanted after about **25 years**).

*Examples:* plantation crops — oil palm, rubber, cocoa, tea, coffee, coconut; fruit crops — durian, rambutan, jackfruit, mango, star fruit; beverage crops — coffee, tea, cocoa.

![Durian, a classic perennial fruit crop](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Durian%20Fruit.JPG?width=600)
*Image: "Durian Fruit" by Ismael Cacharro, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).*

**Perennial crops managed as short-cycle crops:** some botanically perennial species are commercially managed on a 1–2 year production cycle:
- **Banana** (*Musa* spp.) — a perennial herb; new suckers give continuous growth, but plots are commercially replanted or renewed after each cycle.
- **Pineapple** (*Ananas comosus*) — herbaceous perennial; commercial cycle is commonly ~12–18 months.
- **Sugarcane** (*Saccharum officinarum*) — a perennial grass, managed through repeated **ratooning** (regrowth from cut stubble).

### Why the distinction matters

| | Annual crops | Perennial crops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Life cycle | Short (< 1 year) | Long (> 2 years) |
| Returns | Quick, frequent harvests | Long-term investment, sustained production |
| Role | Food security, daily food supply | Export earnings, economic growth |
| Flexibility | Easy to switch crops with market demand | Continuous income over many years |
| Land use | Flexible crop rotation | Permanent ground cover; supports carbon sequestration |
| Examples | Paddy, maize, vegetables | Oil palm, rubber, coconut, fruit trees |

Annual and perennial crops play **complementary roles**: annuals secure short-term food supply while perennials provide long-term economic and environmental benefit. Both can be further classified by their **use/commodity**.

## 3.3 Classification by Use / Commodity

According to use, crops fall into several (often overlapping) categories:

| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Food crops** | Paddy, maize, tapioca, sweet potato, sago (temperate: potato, wheat, barley, oats) |
| **Plantation/industrial crops** | Oil palm, rubber, coconut |
| **Beverage crops** | Cocoa, coffee, tea |
| **Medicinal plants** | Noni (mengkudu), misai kucing, kacip fatimah, tongkat ali, hempedu bumi |
| **Spices** | Clove, cinnamon (kayu manis), turmeric (kunyit), ginger |
| **Fibre crops** | Cotton, kenaf, kekabu, jute |
| **Oil crops** | Peanut, soybean, sunflower, canola, coconut, oil palm |
| **Forage/pasture crops** | Napier, guinea grass, setaria grass |
| **Leguminous cover crops** | Centrosema, pueraria, calopogonium |
| **Forest species** | Sentang, jati, keruing, rasak, cengal, nyatoh |
| **Horticulture crops** | Vegetables (sawi, bayam, tomato, long bean, chilli, salad); fruits (durian, rambutan, ciku, cempedak, dragon fruit, banana); ornamentals (bougainvillea, hibiscus, orchid, rose); turf grasses (Bermuda grass, pearl grass) |

**A crop does not belong to only one category** — it depends on the purpose of classification:

| Crop | Life cycle | Commodity/use | Horticulture? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 🌳 Durian | Perennial | Fruit crop | Yes (Pomology) |
| 🍅 Tomato | Annual | Vegetable crop | Yes (Olericulture) |
| 🍫 Cocoa | Perennial | Beverage / industrial crop | Usually plantation crop |
| 🌴 Oil palm | Perennial | Oil / industrial crop | No |
| 🥥 Coconut | Perennial | Oil / food / plantation crop | Borderline (often plantation) |
| 🌿 Misai kucing | Perennial herb | Medicinal crop | Yes |

### Rice (*Oryza sativa*) — Malaysia's major food crop
- The **main staple food** for Malaysians and much of Asia, and central to national food security.
- Malaysia produces about **60–70%** of its own rice requirement; the rest is imported (Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Myanmar, India).
- Grown on an estate basis with irrigation, allowing **2 harvests a year (or 5 in 2 years)**; direct seeding and combine-harvester harvesting are common.
- **Top paddy producers (2024 est., million tonnes):** China (208), India (196), Bangladesh (57), Indonesia (55), Vietnam (43) — Malaysia is not among the top 10.
- **Local varieties:** MR219 (widely grown, high-yielding), MR220CL2 (herbicide-tolerant), MR297/Siraj 297 (high yield & quality), MR315/Seri Waja (short maturity, 105–109 days), MRQ104/Kembang Sari (fragrant specialty rice); historical varieties Malinja (1964), Bahagia (1968), Mahsuri (1965); UPM's new **Padi Putra 1 & 2** are Climate-Ready (CR) varieties.

**Other key food crops:** **Cassava** (*Manihot esculenta*) → chips, snacks, flour; **Maize** (*Zea mays*) → food and livestock feed; **Sago** (*Metroxylon sagu*) → major starch crop in Sarawak, with waste used for livestock feed. Malaysia imports large amounts of maize and soybean to supply its animal feed industry.

### Oil palm (*Elaeis guineensis*) — Malaysia's "Golden Crop"
- Introduced to Malaysia from West Africa in **1871** (first grown as an ornamental); the first commercial plantation was established in **1917** at Tennamaram Estate, Kuala Selangor.
- Malaysia is the **world's 2nd-largest producer/exporter of palm oil (CPO)**, after Indonesia — a major source of export tax revenue and employment.
- Planted area, 2025: **5.70 million ha** nationally (Peninsular 2.54 m ha, Sabah 1.50 m ha, Sarawak 1.66 m ha), up 1.6% from 2024 (Source: MPOB).
- Called the **"Golden Crop"** for the golden colour of its fruit and the large profits/export revenue it generates.
- **Tenera** (a Dura × Pisifera F1 hybrid) is the most widely planted type: **thin shell**, thick oil-rich mesocarp, high fresh-fruit-bunch (FFB) yield; fruits from **2–3 years** after planting; commercial lifespan **25–30 years**.

![Oil palm tree bearing a fresh fruit bunch](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Buah%20kelapa%20sawit%20%2821%29.JPG?width=600)
*Image: "Buah kelapa sawit" (oil palm fresh fruit bunch) by Wagino 20100516, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

### Rubber (*Hevea brasiliensis*)
- Family Euphorbiaceae; originated in the **Amazon Valley, Brazil**. Seeds were brought to Malaysia in **1877** by Mr. Ridley and planted in Kuala Kangsar; estate planting began 1902/1903. Malaysia was once the world's largest natural-rubber producer (1.8 million ha planted in the 1990s).
- Malaysia is a world leader in rubber-based manufacturing — examination/surgical gloves, tyres, catheters, condoms, latex threads.
- Trees can be **tapped ~4 years after planting** (trunk girth ~45 cm at 150 cm height); latex is collected via a tapping panel into cups.

| Product | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Latex | Fresh liquid rubber collected from tapping |
| Cup lump | Coagulated rubber formed naturally in collection cups |
| SMR (Standard Malaysian Rubber) | High-quality graded rubber for tyre/rubber industries |
| RSS (Ribbed Smoked Sheet) | Rolled, dried & smoke-treated rubber sheet |
| ADS (Air-Dried Sheet) | Rubber sheet dried without smoking |
| Scrap rubber | Lower-grade leftover rubber |

**Rubber clones** are selected planting material bred for higher latex yield, growth, disease tolerance and timber value: **latex clones** (e.g. RRIM 928, RRIM 2001, PB 260) vs **latex-timber clones** (e.g. RRIM 2000 series, RRIM 3001) that yield both latex and valuable rubberwood.

![Tapping a rubber tree to collect latex](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Rubber%20tapping%20%2848058%29.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Rubber tapping" by Manukrishnan80, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).*

### Coconut (*Cocos nucifera*)
- A perennial plantation crop grown mainly by **smallholders** in Malaysia (>200,000 ha), tolerant of coastal sandy (BRIS) soils and inland areas.
- Challenges: many palms are **ageing (>40 years)**, giving lower yields and difficult (tall-palm) harvesting — replanting with improved varieties is a priority.
- Called the **"Tree of Life"** — almost every part is usable: fruit (coconut water, santan, desiccated coconut), kernel (coconut oil, virgin coconut oil), and other parts (coir fibre from husk, activated carbon from shell, animal feed).
- **Improved varieties** (bred by MARDI/Dept. of Agriculture) are shorter and easier to harvest: Malayan Tall (traditional, tall, long-lived) vs dwarf types — Malayan Yellow Dwarf (MYD), Malayan Red Dwarf (MRD), Pandan (aromatic water), and the high-yielding **MATAG hybrid** (Malayan Dwarf × Tagnanan Tall — higher yield, earlier fruiting, shorter palm, suited to commercial production).

### Fibre and forage crops
Grown mainly for non-food industrial or feed purposes:
- **Kenaf** (*Hibiscus cannabinus*, family Malvaceae) — fast-growing annual fibre crop. Stem fibre → paper/pulp, biodegradable packaging, composites; core fibre → animal bedding, absorbents, bio-based products. Promoted as a renewable alternative crop.
- **Forage sorghum** (*Sorghum bicolor*) — annual grass grown as animal feed; fast-growing, drought-tolerant, high biomass. Used fresh, as silage, or for bioenergy/industrial biomass.

## 3.4 Horticulture Crops in Detail

**Horticulture** is the branch of agriculture concerned with cultivating and growing plants for both **consumption** and **aesthetic/ornamental purposes**. It covers an unusually wide range of plants and practices, spanning the full chain from planting and harvesting to storage, processing and sale, and can be scaled up and commercialised like any other branch of agriculture.

| Branch | Focus | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Olericulture** | Growing crops for consumption/food, including planting, harvesting, storage, processing and marketing | Cabbage, asparagus, tomatoes |
| **Pomology** | The science of fruit and nut crops — large, small, soft, hard, seeded or colourful | Apple, orange, peanut, mango |
| **Viticulture** | Cultivation and harvesting of grapes, mostly for winemaking; a major industry in the US, Canada, Italy, France | Grapes |
| **Floriculture** | Growing and marketing flowers/ornamental plants, incl. flower arrangement; often produced in temperate greenhouse structures | Rose, dahlia, chrysanthemum, sunflower |
| **Turf** | The dense, matted grass surface layer of soil, grown for ornamental or recreational use | Bermuda grass, Mutiara grass |
| **Landscape** | Arranging ornamental plants and garden materials to create a picturesque, naturalistic effect | Garden/landscape design |

![Hibiscus, a common floriculture / ornamental crop](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Hibiscus%20rosa-sinensis%20blossom.jpg?width=600)
*Image: "Hibiscus rosa-sinensis blossom" by Kreuzschnabel, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

Within the "use/commodity" system (Section 3.3), horticulture crops were already grouped into **vegetables, fruit crops, ornamentals and turf grasses** — the branches above (olericulture, pomology, floriculture, turf) are simply the *scientific disciplines* that study and manage each of those groups.

## 3.5 Botanical & Morphological Classification

**Plant morphology** is the study of a plant's physical form and external structure — leaf shape, floral structure, fruit shape/size, trunk type, seed shape and colour. **Botanical naming** gives every plant a unique two-part Latin name (genus + species) recognised worldwide, regardless of local/common names.

Crops with similar botanical characteristics are grouped through a taxonomic hierarchy:

**Order → Group (Dicot/Monocot) → Family → Sub-family → Genus → Species (spp.) → Cultivar/variety/clone**

At the top level, the **Plant Kingdom** splits into:
- **Angiosperms** (flowering plants) → **Dicotyledons** (e.g. durian, rubber) or **Monocotyledons** (e.g. maize, palms, bamboo, pandan)
- **Gymnosperms** (non-flowering plants)

**Worked examples** (commercial/planted clones):

| Rank | Durian, clone D24 | Cempedak, clone CH1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Order | Angiosperm | Angiosperm |
| Group | Dicotyledon | Dicotyledon |
| Family | Malvaceae / Bombacaceae | Moraceae |
| Genus | *Durio* | *Artocarpus* |
| Species (botanical name) | *Durio zibethinus* | *Artocarpus integer* |
| Variety/clone | D24 | CH1 |
| Full scientific reference | Durian (*Durio zibethinus* var. D24) | Cempedak (*Artocarpus integer* var. CH1) |

*In theses, scientific papers and reports, both the common name and the full botanical name (with variety/clone) should be given whenever a species is mentioned.*

### Monocots vs dicots
The two groups of flowering plants are told apart by several structural features:

| Feature | Monocot | Dicot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Leaf venation** | Veins usually parallel | Veins usually netlike |
| **Stems** | Vascular tissue scattered | Vascular tissue arranged in a ring |
| **Flowers** | Floral organs usually in multiples of three | Floral organs usually in multiples of four or five |
| **Roots** | Root system usually fibrous (no main root) | Taproot (main root) usually present |
| **Embryo** | One cotyledon | Two cotyledons |
| **Pollen** | Pollen grain with one opening | Pollen grain with three openings |

![Comparison of monocot and dicot plant characteristics](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Monocot_vs_Dicot.svg?width=600)
*Image: "Monocot vs Dicot" by Flowerpower207, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

Beyond species, crops are further distinguished by **cultivars, varieties and clones** — named, selected planting material (e.g. durian clone D24, rubber clone RRIM 928, rice variety MR219) chosen for specific traits such as yield, disease resistance or fruit quality. **Clones** are propagated from live plant material (buds, cuttings, tissue culture) and are genetically identical to the parent, while **varieties** typically arise from pollinated seed.

## Summary

- Crops are classified by **three main approaches**: life cycle, use/commodity, and botanical/morphological characteristics — and a single crop can fall under several systems at once.
- **Life cycle:** annual (< 1 year, e.g. paddy, maize), biennial (2 years, vegetative then reproductive year, e.g. carrot, cabbage), perennial (> 2 years, e.g. oil palm, rubber, durian); some perennials (banana, pineapple, sugarcane) are managed on short commercial cycles.
- **Use/commodity:** food, plantation/industrial, beverage, medicinal, spice, fibre, oil, forage, cover crop, forest species, and horticulture crops — Malaysia's key examples are **rice** (staple food), **oil palm** ("Golden Crop," Tenera hybrid), **rubber** (gloves, tyres, RSS/SMR products) and **coconut** ("Tree of Life," MATAG hybrid).
- **Horticulture** covers plants grown for both food and aesthetics, split into six branches: **olericulture** (vegetables), **pomology** (fruit/nuts), **viticulture** (grapes), **floriculture** (flowers/ornamentals), **turf** (lawn grasses), and **landscape** (garden design).
- **Botanical classification** follows Order → Group (Dicot/Monocot) → Family → Genus → Species → Cultivar; monocots and dicots differ in leaf venation, stem vascular arrangement, floral parts, root type, cotyledon number and pollen openings.
