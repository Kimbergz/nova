## 1.1 Matter

**Matter** is anything that possesses **mass** and **occupies volume**.

- Matter exists in **three states**: solid, liquid, gas (plus plasma, not covered here).
- It includes everything around us — the air we breathe, our skin and bones, the earth beneath us.
- Whether a substance is solid, liquid, or gas depends on how closely its particles are packed and how much energy (temperature) they have — **higher temperature** generally moves matter from solid → liquid → gas.

## 1.2 Classification of Matter

Matter is first split into **pure substances** and **mixtures**, and each of those splits further.

### Pure substance vs. mixture

| | Pure substance | Mixture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | A single element or single compound, entirely free of other components | Two or more substances physically combined, not chemically bonded |
| **Composition** | Fixed / specific | Variable |
| **Made of** | Only one type of atom or molecule | Two or more pure substances |
| **Separation** | Cannot be separated by physical methods | Can be separated by physical methods |
| **Sub-types** | Elements or compounds | Homogeneous or heterogeneous |
| **Examples** | Pure iron (Fe), sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) | Salt water, a mixture of salt and charcoal |

### Elements vs. compounds

| | Elements | Compounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | Pure substances made of only **one type of atom** | Pure substances made of **two or more elements** chemically bonded in a fixed ratio |
| **Breakdown** | Cannot be broken down by chemical reaction | **Can** be broken down by chemical reaction |
| **Examples** | Sodium (Na), chlorine gas (Cl₂), ozone (O₃) | Salt (NaCl), table sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁), water (H₂O) |

- **Monatomic** elements exist as single atoms: He, Au, Na.
- **Diatomic** elements exist as pairs: O₂, H₂, Cl₂.
- **Polyatomic** elements are more complex: O₃ (ozone), S₈, P₄.
- The terms "substance" and "compound" are often used interchangeably.

> **Quick check:** In F₂, NaSO₄, O₂, CO, Li, CO₂ — **Li** is the only atom; **F₂, O₂** are elements (molecules); **NaSO₄, CO, CO₂** are compounds; all six are molecules except Li.

### Homogeneous vs. heterogeneous mixtures

| | Homogeneous | Heterogeneous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition** | Same composition throughout | Composition **varies** throughout |
| **Components** | Indistinguishable, not visible as separate parts | Distinguishable, visible as separate parts |
| **Examples** | Salt water, air, bronze (a solid solution) | Sand, oil and water |

![Comparison of homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Heterogeneous_and_Homogeneous_mixtures.svg?width=320)
*Image: "Heterogeneous and Homogeneous mixtures" by Mvolz, Wikimedia Commons.*

> **Try it:** classify each as homogeneous or heterogeneous — stone, copper sulphate solution, granite, sugar in water, energy drinks, vinegar, air, wood, stainless steel, brass, whole milk, concrete, gravel.
> *(Solutions like copper sulphate, sugar-water, vinegar, air, stainless steel, and brass are homogeneous; stone, granite, wood, whole milk, concrete, and gravel are heterogeneous.)*

## 1.3 Physical and Chemical Properties

Matter shows two broad categories of properties:

- **Physical property** — observed/measured **without** changing the substance's composition; no new substance is formed.
- **Chemical property** — describes how a substance **reacts**; a new substance with different composition is formed.

### Physical changes

A **physical change** alters form but **not** chemical composition. Physical properties commonly altered include mass, colour, texture, shape, temperature, density, melting point, and state of matter.

- **Boiling water** — chemical composition stays H₂O; molecules simply move farther apart.
- **Heating an iron bar red-hot** — still chemically the same iron.
- **Dissolving sugar in water** — both sugar and water keep their chemical identity.

### Chemical changes

A **chemical change** occurs when:
1. One or more substances are (at least partly) used up.
2. One or more new substances are formed.
3. Energy is absorbed or released.

- **Iron rusting**: 4 Fe(s) + 3 O₂(g) + 6 H₂O(l) → 4 Fe(OH)₃(s) — iron atoms combine with oxygen and water to form a new substance, rust.
- **Combustion of propane**: atoms rearrange into new substances with very different properties from the starting materials.
- **Mercury + iodine → mercury(II) iodide**: a liquid metal and a black solid react to form a red crystalline solid.

> **Sort these:** striking a match (chemical) · baking soda fizzing in water (chemical) · steel wool burning (chemical) · melting ice (physical) · burning a wooden stick (chemical) · dissolving salt in water (physical) · sodium reacting violently in water (chemical).

## 1.4 Units of Measurement

A **measurement** is a specific characteristic of an object/event that can be compared with others. Every measurement needs **two pieces of information**: a **number** (quantitative) and a **unit** (qualitative) — "8 seconds" and "8 hours" are worlds apart.

### Two unit systems

- **Imperial system** — ounces, pounds; inches, feet, yards, miles; cups, pints, gallons. Still used in Britain/USA day-to-day, but dropped from scientific work since the 1960s.
- **SI units** (Système Internationale) — the modern Metric System, used globally in science, industry, and commerce.

### The six SI base units used in chemistry

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Abbreviation |
| -------- | ------ | ---- | ------------ |
| Length | *l* | metre | m |
| Mass | *m* | kilogram | kg |
| Time | *t* | second | s |
| Electric current | *I* | ampere | A |
| Temperature | *T* | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | *n* | mole | mol |

![The SI base units](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/SI_base_units.svg?width=320)
*Image: "SI base units," Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

Two **"honorary" units** are also acceptable alongside the base units:

| Unit | Symbol | Relationship |
| ---- | ------ | ------------ |
| Litre | L | 1 L = 1 dm³ = 1000 mL |
| Atomic mass unit | amu (u) | 1 amu = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg |

**Derived units** combine base units:

| Quantity | Derived unit | Symbol |
| -------- | ------------ | ------ |
| Density | — | kg m⁻³ |
| Electric charge | coulomb | C (A s) |
| Energy | joule | J (kg m² s⁻²) |
| Force | newton | N (kg m s⁻²) |
| Frequency | hertz | Hz (s⁻¹) |
| Pressure | pascal / atmosphere / torr / mmHg | Pa (N m⁻²) / atm / torr / mmHg |
| Velocity | metre per second | m s⁻¹ |
| Volume | cubic metre | m³ |

### Metric prefixes

| Prefix | Symbol | Power of 10 | Multiplier |
| ------ | ------ | ----------- | ---------- |
| kilo | k | 10³ | × 1,000 |
| deci | d | 10⁻¹ | × 0.1 |
| centi | c | 10⁻² | × 0.01 |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ | × 0.001 |
| micro | μ | 10⁻⁶ | × 0.000001 |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ | × 0.000000001 |

> **Worked example:** 1 g = 10⁻³ kg = 10¹ dg = 10² cg = 10³ mg = 10⁶ μg = 10⁹ ng. The same pattern applies to 1 m in km, dm, cm, mm, μm, nm.

## 1.5 Uncertainty in Measurement

Every measured number comes from a measuring device, and scientists must record it honestly:

- Record **all certain digits, plus one estimated (uncertain) digit**.
- The **last digit** of any measurement is always the uncertain/estimated one.
- Example: a burette reading of **26.35 mL** — "26.3" is known with certainty, and the final "5" is estimated.

### Decimal places vs. significant figures

For 26.35: **2 decimal places**, **4 significant figures**. More significant figures = more certain the measurement.

> **Worked examples**
> - A measurement of **7.25 m** — all digits significant; the **5** is the uncertain one.
> - A measurement of **87 seconds** — both digits significant; the **7** is the uncertain one.
> - Reading a 25 mL graduated cylinder: the first two digits (2 and 1) are 100% certain, and the third digit is estimated between the smallest markings — e.g. **21.6 mL**, where the final "6" is the uncertain digit but still counts as significant.

The uncertainty of a measurement always depends on the **precision of the instrument**. Pipettes and volumetric flasks are high-precision glassware calibrated to deliver/contain **exactly one volume**, marked by a single graduation line — so they carry much less uncertainty than a beaker or measuring cylinder.

## 1.6 Accuracy and Precision

Experimental measurements should be as accurate and precise as possible, with the least possible error. Picture a dartboard: hitting near the bullseye is **accurate**; hitting the same spot repeatedly (even off-centre) is **precise**.

### Accuracy

**Accuracy** = how close a measurement is to the **theoretical (accepted) value**.

$$\%\ \text{error} = \frac{|\text{theoretical value} - \text{experimental value}|}{\text{theoretical value}} \times 100\%$$

High accuracy ↔ low % error; low accuracy ↔ high % error. The *experimental value* is the average of repeated measurements; the *theoretical value* is what's expected under ideal conditions.

### Precision

**Precision** = how close repeated measurements are **to each other**, measured by **standard deviation**. High precision ↔ low standard deviation; low precision ↔ high standard deviation.

> **Worked example:** the theoretical mass is 100.00 g.
> - **Set A:** 99.98, 100.01, 99.99, 99.98 g → average = **99.99 g** → % error = |100.00 − 99.99| / 100.00 × 100 = **0.01%** (very accurate); values are tightly clustered → very precise.
> - **Set B:** 100.10, 100.00, 99.88, 99.92 g → average = **99.975 g** → % error ≈ **0.025%**; the values are more spread out → less precise than Set A.
>
> So Set A is both **more accurate and more precise**.

## 1.7 Handling Numbers

### Scientific notation

Scientific notation uses powers of 10 to avoid ambiguity with very large or very small numbers.

$$10^1=10,\quad 10^2=100,\quad 10^3=1{,}000 \qquad\qquad 10^{-1}=0.1,\quad 10^{-2}=0.01,\quad 10^{-3}=0.001$$

> **Converting to scientific notation:** 720000 = 7.2 × 10⁵ · 0.093267 = 9.3267 × 10⁻² · 0.062 = 6.2 × 10⁻² · 0.0940 = 9.40 × 10⁻²

### Rounding off

1. Identify the rounding digit and look at the digit to its right.
2. If that digit is **< 5**, the rounding digit stays the same (8.64 → 8.6).
3. If that digit is **≥ 5**, the rounding digit increases by 1 (8.65 → 8.7; 8.68 → 8.7).

### Significant figures — the rules

A **significant figure** is a digit that expresses a result to its true measured precision (ignore the decimal point when counting — just count digits left to right).

| Rule | Detail | Example | Sig. figs |
| ---- | ------ | ------- | --------- |
| 1. Non-zero digits | Always significant | 38.25 cm | 4 |
| 2a. Leading zeroes | **Not** significant (placeholders before the number starts) | 0.008 cm | 1 |
| 2b. Captive zeroes | Zeroes **between** non-zero digits — **always** significant | 50.6 mm | 3 |
| 2c. Trailing zeroes (no decimal point) | **Not** significant — placeholders only | 25,000 mm | 2 |
| 2c′. Trailing zeroes (with decimal point) | **Are** significant | 2.00 kg | 3 |

### Significant figures in calculations

- **Addition/subtraction:** the answer keeps only as many **decimal places** as the **least precise** number used.
  - e.g. 2.54 g + 2.201 g + 2.6345 g = 7.3755 g → rounds to **7.38 g** (matching 2.54's 2 decimal places).
- **Multiplication/division:** the answer keeps only as many **significant figures** as the number with the **fewest sig. figs.**

> **Practice:** round each to the correct number of sig. figs / decimal places — 315.05 + 19.6 + 1.1 · 4.231 + 3.51 · 45300 + 176.21 · 3.542 − 2.1 · 6.357 − 2.47 · 8.4 × 5 · 2.19 × 4.2 · 279 × 83 · 32.6 ÷ 2.8 · 464.6895 ÷ 12.145.
> *(Apply the two rules above: for + / − match the least number of decimal places; for × / ÷ match the least number of significant figures.)*

## 1.8 Exact Numbers

An **exact number** has **no uncertainty** and, in principle, an **infinite number of significant figures** — it never limits the sig. figs. in a calculation. Exact numbers arise from:

- **Counting** — e.g. "8 beakers" (the number of beakers is counted, not measured).
- **Conversion factors defined by definition** — e.g. 1 atm = 760 mmHg.
- **The word "percent"** — the 100 in a percentage calculation.
- **A denominator used for an average** — e.g. dividing a sum by the number of trials.
- **Defined physical constants** — e.g. the gas constant R = 8.3145 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; the specific heat of water = 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

> **Worked example:** 8 beakers of KOH, each with the same mass; one beaker weighs 27.3 g. Total mass = 27.3 g × 8 (exact) = 218.4 g → rounds to **218 g** (3 sig. figs, limited by 27.3 g, not by the exact "8").

## 1.9 Dimensional Analysis

A **conversion factor** is a numerical factor used to multiply or divide a quantity when converting between unit systems — e.g. by definition, 1 kg = 1000 g (exactly).

- In dimensional analysis, units are carried through **every step** of a calculation.
- If the equation is set up correctly, all the unwanted units **cancel out**, leaving only the desired unit.
- This is the standard method for converting between units (e.g. g ↔ kg, mL ↔ L, seconds ↔ hours) and for chemistry calculations that chain several conversions together (mass → moles → particles, etc. — covered in Chapter 2).

## Quick recap

- **Matter** has mass and occupies volume, and exists as solid, liquid, or gas.
- Matter splits into **pure substances** (elements, compounds) and **mixtures** (homogeneous, heterogeneous).
- **Physical changes** keep chemical composition the same; **chemical changes** create new substances.
- Measurements need a **number + unit**; chemistry uses **SI units**, honorary units (L, amu), derived units, and metric prefixes.
- Every measurement has one **uncertain (estimated) digit** — precision of the reading depends on the instrument.
- **Accuracy** = closeness to the true value (% error); **precision** = closeness of repeated readings to each other (standard deviation).
- **Significant figures**: non-zero digits and captive zeroes always count; leading zeroes never count; trailing zeroes count only with a decimal point.
- **Addition/subtraction** → match decimal places; **multiplication/division** → match significant figures.
- **Exact numbers** (counted items, defined conversions, percentages, constants) never limit significant figures.
- **Dimensional analysis** carries units through a calculation so they cancel, leaving the desired unit.
