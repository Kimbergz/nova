## 2.1 Subatomic Particles and Isotopes

An atom is built from **three subatomic particles**: **electrons**, **protons**, and **neutrons**.

- In a **neutral atom**, the number of protons **exactly equals** the number of electrons, so the total charge is **zero**.
- If an atom **loses** electrons → the resulting ion is **positively** charged (cation).
- If an atom **gains** electrons → the resulting ion is **negatively** charged (anion).

### Isotopic notation

- **Proton number (Z)** — number of protons; defines the element.
- **Nucleon number (A)**, also called mass number — protons + neutrons.
- **Isotopes** — atoms of the same element (same proton number) with **different numbers of neutrons** (different nucleon numbers).

> **Worked example — mass of an isotope:** carbon always has 6 protons. Isotopes with 6, 7, and 8 neutrons have nucleon numbers 12, 13, and 14 respectively → carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14.

> **Worked example — counting particles:** for sodium, proton number = 11, nucleon number = 23.
>
> | Species | Nucleons | Protons | Electrons | Neutrons |
> | ------- | -------- | ------- | --------- | -------- |
> | Na | 23 | 11 | 11 | 23 − 11 = 12 |
> | Na⁺ | 23 | 11 | 11 − 1 = 10 | 23 − 11 = 12 |
>
> Losing an electron changes the electron count, but **never** the proton or neutron count.

## 2.2 Average Atomic Mass

A **mass spectrum** shows the isotopes of an element as peaks — the peak **position** gives the isotopic mass, and the peak **intensity (height)** gives the relative abundance.

- Example — uranium has three isotopes: ²³⁴U, ²³⁵U, ²³⁸U with abundances 0.01%, 0.72%, and 99.27% respectively. ²³⁴U has the smallest peak (rarest); ²³⁸U has the largest peak (most abundant).
- Example — oxygen has three isotopes with masses 15.995, 16.995, and 17.995, and abundances 99.757%, 0.038%, and 0.205%.

The **average atomic mass** (the value on the periodic table) is the **weighted average** of all naturally occurring isotopes, reported to **two decimal places**:

$$\text{average atomic mass} = \sum (\text{isotopic mass} \times \text{fractional abundance})$$

> **Worked example:** chromium has isotopes 50, 52, 53, 54 amu with abundances 0.0435, 0.8379, 0.0950, 0.0236.
> $$(50\times0.0435)+(52\times0.8379)+(53\times0.0950)+(54\times0.0236) = 52.06\ \text{amu}$$

> **Worked example (working backwards to abundance):** silver has two isotopes, ¹⁰⁷Ag (106.9051 amu) and ¹⁰⁹Ag (108.9048 amu); the average atomic mass is 107.94 amu. Letting *x* = fractional abundance of ¹⁰⁷Ag:
> $$106.9051x + 108.9048(1-x) = 107.94 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 0.4825$$
> So ¹⁰⁷Ag ≈ **48.25%** and ¹⁰⁹Ag ≈ **51.75%**.

## 2.3 The Mole Concept

The **mole (mol)** is the SI unit for the amount of a chemical substance — atoms, molecules, ions, electrons, or formula units.

- **1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles** (Avogadro's number, N_A), historically defined from the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.
- The **molar mass** of a substance (g/mol) is numerically equal to its atomic or molecular mass, and acts as the **conversion factor** between mass (g) and amount (mol) of that substance.

$$n\ (\text{mol}) = \frac{\text{mass (g)}}{\text{molar mass (g mol}^{-1})} \qquad\qquad \text{number of particles} = n \times N_A$$

Chemical formulas give the mole ratio between atoms and molecules — e.g. **1 mol of CO₂** contains **1 mol of C atoms** and **2 mol of O atoms** (and therefore 3 mol of atoms total).

## 2.4 Moles, Mass, Particles, and Gas Volume

At **room temperature** and at **STP** (standard temperature and pressure), 1 mole of any gas occupies a fixed **molar volume**, which lets you interconvert between moles and gas volume the same way molar mass links moles and mass:

$$\text{mass} \;\xrightarrow[\div\ \text{molar mass}]{\times\ \text{molar mass}}\; \text{moles} \;\xrightarrow[\div\ N_A]{\times\ N_A}\; \text{number of particles}$$
$$\text{moles of gas} \;\xrightarrow[\div\ \text{molar volume}]{\times\ \text{molar volume}}\; \text{volume of gas}$$

This chain (mass ↔ moles ↔ particles ↔ gas volume) is the core toolkit used throughout stoichiometry in Chapter 3.

## 2.5 Empirical and Molecular Formula

- **Empirical formula** — the **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms in a compound.
- **Molecular formula** — the **actual** number of atoms of each element in one molecule; it is a **whole-number multiple** of the empirical formula.

**Method (from % composition):**
1. Assume a 100 g sample, so each mass % becomes a mass in grams.
2. Convert each mass to moles (divide by atomic molar mass).
3. Divide every mole value by the smallest one to get a whole-number mole ratio → this gives the **empirical formula**.
4. Find the empirical formula's molar mass, then divide the compound's actual molar mass by it to get the multiplier, *n*.
5. Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by *n* to get the **molecular formula**.

> **Worked example:** Compound Z is 85.62% C and 14.38% H by mass; its molar mass is 42.08 g/mol.
> - mol C = 85.62 / 12.01 = 7.13; mol H = 14.38 / 1.01 = 14.24
> - Ratio H : C = 14.24 / 7.13 ≈ **2** → empirical formula **CH₂** (empirical mass ≈ 14.03 g/mol)
> - *n* = 42.08 / 14.03 ≈ **3** → molecular formula **C₃H₆** (propene)

> **Worked example (from masses, not %):** compound V contains 19.93 g K, 26.51 g Cr, and 28.55 g O; molar mass = 294.20 g/mol.
> - mol K = 19.93/39.10 = 0.510; mol Cr = 26.51/52.00 = 0.510; mol O = 28.55/16.00 = 1.784
> - Ratio K : Cr : O ≈ 1 : 1 : 3.5 → doubling gives whole numbers → **K₂Cr₂O₇** (potassium dichromate), which matches the given molar mass.

## 2.6 Concentration of Solutions

Chemists express "how concentrated" a solution is in **five** common ways:

| Term | Formula | Units |
| ---- | ------- | ----- |
| **Molarity (M)** | mol solute ÷ L solution | mol L⁻¹ (M) |
| **Molality (m)** | mol solute ÷ kg **solvent** | mol kg⁻¹ |
| **Mole fraction (X)** | mol of component ÷ total mol of all components | none (ratio) |
| **Mass percentage** | (mass solute ÷ mass solution) × 100% | % |
| **Volume percentage** | (volume solute ÷ volume solution) × 100% | % |

- **Molarity** is based on the **volume of the whole solution** — the most common unit in the lab.
- **Molality** is based on the **mass of solvent only**, so it doesn't change with temperature (unlike molarity, since volume expands/contracts with heat).
- For a solution of components A, B, C: $X_A = \dfrac{n_A}{n_A+n_B+n_C}$, and all mole fractions in a solution add up to 1.
- A **standard solution** is one with an accurately known concentration, prepared by dissolving a precisely weighed mass of solute and making it up to a fixed volume in a volumetric flask.

> **Worked example — combining several concentration units:** a stock solution is 0.92% NaCl by mass, density 1.00 g/cm³ (molar mass NaCl = 58.50 g/mol). Taking 100 g of solution as the basis (0.92 g NaCl + 99.08 g water):
> - mol NaCl = 0.92/58.50 = 0.01573 mol; mol H₂O = 99.08/18.02 = 5.499 mol
> - **Mole fraction of NaCl** = 0.01573 / (0.01573 + 5.499) ≈ **2.85 × 10⁻³**
> - **Molality** = 0.01573 mol ÷ 0.09908 kg water ≈ **0.159 mol/kg**
> - Since density = 1.00 g/cm³, 100 g solution = 100 mL, giving **molarity ≈ 0.157 M**

## 2.7 Dilution of Solutions

**Dilution** reduces a solution's concentration by adding more solvent. The **number of moles of solute stays constant** before and after dilution, so:

$$M_{\text{conc}}V_{\text{conc}} = M_{\text{dil}}V_{\text{dil}}$$

> **Worked example (continuing above):** how much of the 0.157 M NaCl stock solution is needed to prepare 50 mL of 0.1 M NaCl?
> $$V_{\text{conc}} = \frac{M_{\text{dil}}V_{\text{dil}}}{M_{\text{conc}}} = \frac{0.1 \times 50}{0.157} \approx 31.8\ \text{mL} \;(0.0318\ \text{L})$$

## Quick recap

- Atoms are made of **protons, neutrons, electrons**; **isotopes** share proton number but differ in neutron number.
- **Average atomic mass** = the abundance-weighted average of an element's isotopes, from mass-spectrum data.
- **1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles** (Avogadro's number); **molar mass** converts between grams and moles.
- **Empirical formula** = simplest ratio; **molecular formula** = empirical formula × a whole-number multiplier found from the actual molar mass.
- Concentration can be expressed as **molarity, molality, mole fraction, mass %,** or **volume %** — each with a different "whole" in the denominator (solution volume, solvent mass, total moles, or solution mass/volume).
- **Dilution:** $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$ — moles of solute don't change, only the volume they're dissolved in.
