## 3.1 Chemical Equations

**Stoichiometry** is the study of the quantities of substances that react and the quantities of products formed — from the Greek *stoicheion* (element) + *metron* (measure).

A **chemical equation** represents a chemical reaction symbolically. For example:

$$2\,H_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2\,H_2O(g)$$

- Both reactants are in the **gas state**; the product is also a gas.
- The single arrow shows the reaction is **non-reversible**.
- The **coefficients** (2, 1, 2) show the mole ratio: 2 mol H₂ reacts with 1 mol O₂ to give 2 mol H₂O.

## 3.2 Oxidation–Reduction (Redox) Reactions

- **Oxidation** — an atom **loses** electron(s).
- **Reduction** — an atom **gains** electron(s).
- A **redox reaction** involves the **transfer of electrons** from one species to another — oxidation and reduction always happen **together**, as a coupled process.

> **Example — iron displaces copper:** an iron nail dipped into blue copper(II) sulfate solution.
> $$Fe(s) + CuSO_4(aq) \rightarrow FeSO_4(aq) + Cu(s)$$
> **Net ionic equation** (spectator SO₄²⁻ cancelled): $Fe(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow Fe^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)$
> Each Fe atom loses 2 electrons (oxidised to Fe²⁺); each Cu²⁺ ion gains 2 electrons (reduced to Cu metal). The blue solution fades as Cu²⁺ is consumed, and reddish copper metal deposits on the nail.

### Oxidation number

The **oxidation number** (oxidation state) is the number of electrons an atom has lost or gained in a substance. Tracking oxidation numbers lets you spot electron transfer even when it isn't obvious from the formula.

**Rules for assigning oxidation numbers:**

| Rule | Species | Example |
| ---- | ------- | ------- |
| 1 | An atom in its elemental form = **0** | Na, H₂, P₄, S₈ → 0 |
| 2 | Sum of oxidation numbers in a **neutral compound** = **0** | NaOH: Na +1, O −2, H +1 → total 0 |
| 3 | The more electronegative element in a **binary compound** gets the negative oxidation number equal to its anion charge | NF₃: F −1, N +3 |
| 4 | **Oxygen** is usually **−2**, except in peroxides (−1) | Na₂O: O −2; H₂O₂: O −1 |
| 5 | **Hydrogen** is usually **+1**, except in metal hydrides (−1) | H₂O: H +1; CaH₂: H −1 |
| 6 | **Fluorine** is always **−1**; other halogens are −1 in binary compounds (unless bonded to O or a lighter halogen) | NaF: F −1; BrF₃: F −1, Br +3 |

> **Worked example:** oxidation state of P in H₃PO₄? Using rule 2: 3(+1) + P + 4(−2) = 0 → **P = +5**.

## 3.3 Oxidising and Reducing Agents

- **Oxidising agent** — causes oxidation in another species and is **itself reduced**; it's an electron **acceptor**.
- **Reducing agent** — causes reduction in another species and is **itself oxidised**; it's an electron **donor**.

> **Example:** Zn(s) + Fe²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Fe(s)
> - Oxidation: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ (Zn is the **reducing agent**)
> - Reduction: Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Fe(s) (Fe²⁺ is the **oxidising agent**)

> **Worked example:** Sn²⁺ + 2 Fe³⁺ → Sn⁴⁺ + 2 Fe²⁺
> - Oxidised species: Sn²⁺ (loses electrons) · Reduced species: Fe³⁺ (gains electrons)
> - Oxidising agent: **Fe³⁺** · Reducing agent: **Sn²⁺**

> **Spectator species:** in 2 Fe(NO₃)₃(aq) + Ni(s) → 2 Fe(NO₃)₂(aq) + Ni(NO₃)₂(aq), Fe³⁺ is reduced to Fe²⁺ and Ni is oxidised to Ni²⁺, while **NO₃⁻** takes no part in the electron transfer — it's the **spectator ion**.

## 3.4 Balancing Chemical Equations

Two methods are used, depending on the type of reaction:

### A. Inspection method (for straightforward equations)

Adjust coefficients until the number of atoms of each element matches on both sides. Start with the most complex molecule (the one with the most atoms).

> **Example:** N₂(g) + H₂(g) → NH₃(g) is unbalanced. Adding coefficients: **N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g)**.
>
> **More examples:**
> - CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(g)
> - Fe₃O₄(s) + 4 H₂(g) → 3 Fe(s) + 4 H₂O(g)

### B. Ion-electron (half-equation) method — for redox equations

**Steps (neutral solution):**
1. Split the reaction into an **oxidation half** and a **reduction half**.
2. Balance all atoms using integer coefficients.
3. Balance the charge on each half by adding electrons.
4. Multiply each half-equation so both sides have the **same number of electrons**.
5. Add the two halves together, cancelling anything that appears on both sides.

> **Example (neutral solution):** Fe(s) + Cu⁺(aq) → Fe³⁺(aq) + Cu(s)
> - Oxidation: Fe(s) → Fe³⁺(aq) + 3e⁻
> - Reduction: (Cu⁺(aq) + e⁻ → Cu(s)) × 3 → 3 Cu⁺(aq) + 3e⁻ → 3 Cu(s)
> - **Overall:** Fe(s) + 3 Cu⁺(aq) → Fe³⁺(aq) + 3 Cu(s)

**Steps (acidic solution)** — extra steps to balance O and H:
1–2. Split into half-equations; balance all atoms **except O and H**.
3. Balance **oxygen** by adding H₂O.
4. Balance **hydrogen** by adding H⁺.
5. Balance charge with electrons; 6–7. equalise and add electrons, then combine.

> **Example (acidic solution):** Fe²⁺(aq) + MnO₄⁻(aq) → Fe³⁺(aq) + Mn²⁺(aq)
> - Oxidation: Fe²⁺(aq) → Fe³⁺(aq) + e⁻ → ×5 → 5 Fe²⁺(aq) → 5 Fe³⁺(aq) + 5e⁻
> - Reduction: 8 H⁺(aq) + MnO₄⁻(aq) + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺(aq) + 4 H₂O(l)
> - **Overall:** 5 Fe²⁺(aq) + 8 H⁺(aq) + MnO₄⁻(aq) → 5 Fe³⁺(aq) + Mn²⁺(aq) + 4 H₂O(l)

**Basic solution** — balance as if acidic first, then **neutralise every H⁺** by adding an equal number of **OH⁻** to both sides (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O), and cancel any water that appears on both sides.

> **Same reaction in basic solution:**
> $$5Fe^{2+}(aq) + MnO_4^-(aq) + 4H_2O(l) \rightarrow 5Fe^{3+}(aq) + Mn^{2+}(aq) + 8OH^-(aq)$$

## 3.5 Stoichiometric Calculations

Every stoichiometry problem follows the same five steps:

1. **Balance** the chemical equation (if not already balanced).
2. Convert given mass/volume into **moles**.
3. Set up the **mole ratio** from the balanced equation's coefficients.
4. Use the mole ratio to calculate moles of the **desired substance**.
5. Convert moles back to **mass/volume**, if needed.

$$\text{mass}_{\text{given}} \rightarrow \text{mol}_{\text{given}} \xrightarrow{\text{mole ratio}} \text{mol}_{\text{desired}} \rightarrow \text{mass}_{\text{desired}}$$

> **Example:** methane burns in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water. Balanced equation:
> $$CH_4(g) + 2\,O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2\,H_2O(l)$$
> From here, moles of CH₄ reacted can be converted to moles (and then mass or volume) of O₂ used, CO₂ produced, or H₂O produced, using the 1 : 2 : 1 : 2 mole ratio.

## 3.6 Limiting and Excess Reagents

- **Limiting reagent** — the reactant that runs out first; it **limits** how much product can form.
- **Excess reagent** — the reactant that is **not** fully used up.

**Steps to identify them:**
1. Balance the equation.
2. Find the moles of **each** reactant available.
3. Pick one product and calculate how much of it **each** reactant could produce, using the mole ratio.
4. Whichever reactant gives the **smaller** amount of product is the **limiting reagent**.

> **Worked example:** 2 Al(s) + 3 CuSO₄(aq) → Al₂(SO₄)₃(aq) + 3 Cu(s), starting with 5.4 g Al (M = 27.0 g/mol) and 15.96 g CuSO₄ (M = 159.6 g/mol).
> - mol Al = 5.4 / 27.0 = **0.200 mol**; mol CuSO₄ = 15.96 / 159.6 = **0.100 mol**
> - Cu producible from Al: 0.200 × (3/2) = **0.300 mol**
> - Cu producible from CuSO₄: 0.100 × (3/3) = **0.100 mol**
> - CuSO₄ gives the smaller amount → **CuSO₄ is the limiting reagent**; Al is in **excess**.
> - Al actually used = 0.100 × (2/3) = 0.0667 mol (1.8 g) → **Al left over = 5.4 − 1.8 = 3.6 g**

## 3.7 Percentage Yield

- **Theoretical yield** — the maximum amount of product possible, calculated from the limiting reagent, assuming the reaction goes to completion.
- **Actual yield** — the amount of pure, dried product actually obtained in the lab (always ≤ theoretical, due to side reactions, loss during transfer/purification, or an incomplete reaction).
- **Percentage yield** — how much of the theoretical yield was actually obtained:

$$\%\ \text{yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%$$

> **Continuing the example above:** theoretical yield of Cu = 0.100 mol × 63.5 g/mol = **6.35 g**. If **5.72 g** of copper was actually recovered:
> $$\%\ \text{yield} = \frac{5.72}{6.35}\times100\% \approx \mathbf{90.1\%}$$

## Quick recap

- A balanced **chemical equation**'s coefficients give the **mole ratio** between reactants and products.
- **Redox reactions** involve electron transfer: oxidation = electron loss, reduction = electron gain — always coupled.
- **Oxidation numbers** are tracked using a fixed set of rules (element = 0, neutral compound sums to 0, O usually −2, H usually +1, F always −1, etc.).
- An **oxidising agent** gets reduced; a **reducing agent** gets oxidised.
- Equations balance by **inspection** (simple reactions) or the **ion-electron method** (redox reactions in neutral, acidic, or basic solution).
- **Stoichiometry**: balance → moles → mole ratio → moles of desired substance → mass/volume.
- The **limiting reagent** produces the least product and determines the **theoretical yield**; **% yield** compares actual yield to that theoretical maximum.
