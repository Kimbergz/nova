## 4.1 The Evolution of Atomic Models

Starting in 1803, a series of experiments built up the modern picture of the atom, one model correcting the last:

| Scientist (year) | Key idea | What it explained |
| ----------------- | -------- | ------------------ |
| **Dalton** (1803) | Dalton's atomic theory | All matter is made of tiny, indivisible atoms; atoms can't be created or destroyed — the foundation of atomic theory |
| **Thomson** (1897) | "Plum pudding" model, from the cathode ray tube experiment | Discovered the **electron**; pictured electrons embedded in a sphere of uniform positive charge |
| **Rutherford** (1911) | Gold foil experiment | Discovered the **positively charged nucleus**, which holds nearly all of an atom's mass, with electrons around it |
| **Bohr** (1913) | Electrons in fixed circular orbits | Explained the **stability** of electron orbits and the **quantisation** of energy levels |
| **Schrödinger** (1926) | Electrons behave as waves | The current model — electrons occupy regions of space (**orbitals**) around the nucleus, described by probability |

## 4.2 Bohr's Atomic Model

In 1913, Niels Bohr proposed a **quantised shell model** to explain how electrons can have stable orbits.

**Bohr's postulates:**
- Electrons revolve in **circular orbits/shells** around the nucleus without absorbing or emitting energy while they stay in that orbit.
- Each orbit (**energy level**) has a fixed, **quantised** amount of energy — so orbits are also called energy levels.
- The energy of an electron in orbit *n* of a hydrogen atom is:

$$E_n = -\frac{R_H}{n^2}$$

where $R_H$ = Rydberg's constant = **2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J**, and *n* = 1, 2, 3, ... The **lowest** energy level, *n* = 1, is the **ground state** — the electron's most stable state.

> **Worked example:** energy of an electron at *n* = 2 in hydrogen:
> $$E_2 = -\frac{2.18\times10^{-18}}{2^2} = -5.45\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$

- At the **ground state**, the electron is most stable (lowest energy level).
- If enough energy is supplied, the electron **absorbs** it and jumps to a **higher** energy level (excited state).
- At an **excited state**, the electron is unstable and **falls back** to a lower level, **releasing** the excess energy as light (a photon).

As the energy level increases, the **frequency** of emitted light increases while the **wavelength** decreases.

## 4.3 Weaknesses of Bohr's Model

Bohr's model successfully predicted the line spectrum of hydrogen (a one-electron system), but it has real limits:

- Cannot explain the line spectra of atoms with **more than one electron**.
- Cannot explain the presence of **multiple, closely-spaced spectral lines**.
- Cannot explain the **dual (wave–particle) nature of matter** (De Broglie's concept).
- Contradicts the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle** — Bohr's fixed, well-defined orbits imply we can know an electron's position and momentum simultaneously, which the uncertainty principle forbids.

## 4.4 Electromagnetic Radiation and Planck's Hypothesis

**Electromagnetic radiation** is a form of energy released and absorbed by charged particles (electrons), showing wave-like behaviour as it travels through space. It is produced whenever an electron's energy level changes.

$$c = \lambda f$$

where *c* = speed of light = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, *λ* = wavelength, *f* = frequency.

In 1900, **Max Planck** proposed that atoms absorb/emit energy only in discrete packets called **quanta**, proportional to frequency:

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

where *h* = Planck's constant = **6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s**.

> **Worked example:** energy of a photon of 250 nm radiation:
> $$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.63\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^{8})}{250\times10^{-9}} \approx 7.96\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$

---

## 4.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The **electromagnetic spectrum** is the full range of electromagnetic radiation, from lowest to highest frequency: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays.

![The electromagnetic spectrum](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Electromagnetic_spectrum_-eng.svg?width=560)
*Image: "Electromagnetic spectrum -eng," Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).*

| | Continuous spectrum | Line spectrum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Content** | Every wavelength in a range | Only a few specific wavelengths |
| **Appearance** | No sharp, discrete lines between colours | Sharp, discrete lines at specific wavelengths |
| **Example** | A rainbow | Emission lines / absorption lines |

Every element has a **unique line spectrum** — like a fingerprint. When light from a gas discharge tube passes through a prism, it splits into these characteristic lines.

## 4.6 Formation of the Line Spectrum of the Hydrogen Atom

- A ground-state H atom has its electron at *n* = 1.
- The electron absorbs energy and jumps to a higher level (**excited state**).
- Being unstable, the electron **falls back** to a lower level, **emitting** light of a specific frequency — this is what produces a line in the spectrum.

The **energy of the light** released in a transition between two levels is:

$$\Delta E = -R_H\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$$

where $n_i$ = initial (higher) level, $n_f$ = final (lower) level.

> **Worked example:** energy released when an electron falls from *n* = 4 to *n* = 1:
> $$\Delta E = -R_H\left(\frac{1}{1^2}-\frac{1}{4^2}\right) = -2.18\times10^{-18}\times0.9375 \approx -2.04\times10^{-18}\ \text{J}$$
> (The negative sign shows energy is **released**.)

The **wavelength** of the emitted light is found from the **Rydberg equation**:

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$$

where *R* = Rydberg constant = **1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹**.

> **Worked example:** wavelength when an electron falls from *n* = 5 to *n* = 2:
> $$\frac{1}{\lambda} = 1.097\times10^7\left(\frac{1}{2^2}-\frac{1}{5^2}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda \approx 4.34\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = \mathbf{434\ nm}$$
> (This falls in the visible blue-violet region — one of the lines of the **Balmer series**, discussed next.)

## 4.7 Spectral Series of the Hydrogen Atom

At low pressure, hydrogen gas in a discharge tube emits light that, viewed through a spectroscope, resolves into distinct **series** of lines. Each series is named for the **final** energy level ($n_f$) the electron falls to:

| Series | Region | $n_f$ | $n_i$ |
| ------ | ------ | ----- | ----- |
| **Lyman** | Ultraviolet | 1 | 2, 3, 4, 5, ... |
| **Balmer** | Visible | 2 | 3, 4, 5, 6, ... |
| **Paschen** | Infrared | 3 | 4, 5, 6, 7, ... |
| **Brackett** | Infrared | 4 | 5, 6, 7, 8, ... |
| **Pfund** | Infrared | 5 | 6, 7, 8, ... |

The **further** an electron falls (the bigger the jump), the **more energy** is released, the **higher the frequency**, and the **shorter the wavelength**.

![The hydrogen emission spectrum, showing the Lyman, Balmer, and Paschen series](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Hydrogen_spectrum.svg?width=520)
*Image: "Hydrogen spectrum" by OrangeDog, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).*

> **Worked examples (first line of each series):**
> - **Lyman** (*n* = 2 → 1): λ ≈ **121.5 nm** (ultraviolet)
> - **Balmer** (*n* = 3 → 2): λ ≈ **656.3 nm** (red, visible light)
> - **Paschen** (*n* = 4 → 3): λ ≈ **1875 nm** (infrared)
>
> All calculated using $\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$.

## 4.8 Ionisation Energy of Hydrogen (from the Lyman Series)

When an electron absorbs enough energy, it feels a progressively **weaker pull** from the nucleus. Once it absorbs enough energy to escape the atom entirely (*n* → ∞), it is **ionised**.

- **Ionisation energy** = the minimum energy needed to remove **1 mole** of electrons from a gaseous atom in its ground state (units: kJ/mol).
- For hydrogen, this corresponds to the Lyman-series transition from *n* = 1 to *n* = ∞:

$$E_{\text{ionisation}} = R_H \times N_A$$

> **Worked example:** using $R_H = 2.18\times10^{-18}$ J and $N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}$ mol⁻¹:
> $$E_{\text{ionisation}} = (2.18\times10^{-18})(6.022\times10^{23}) \approx 1.31\times10^{6}\ \text{J/mol} = \mathbf{1312\ kJ/mol}$$

## Quick recap

- The atomic model evolved: **Dalton** (indivisible particles) → **Thomson** (electron, plum pudding) → **Rutherford** (nucleus) → **Bohr** (quantised orbits) → **Schrödinger** (probability/wave orbitals).
- **Bohr's model**: electrons sit in fixed, quantised circular orbits; $E_n = -R_H/n^2$. It works for hydrogen but breaks down for multi-electron atoms and conflicts with the uncertainty principle.
- **Planck**: energy is absorbed/emitted in quanta, $E = hf = hc/\lambda$.
- The **electromagnetic spectrum** ranges from radio waves to gamma rays; each element's **line spectrum** is a unique fingerprint.
- Electron transitions release light whose energy is $\Delta E = -R_H(1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2)$ and whose wavelength follows the **Rydberg equation**.
- The **Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett,** and **Pfund** series correspond to transitions ending at $n_f$ = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 respectively.
- **Ionisation energy of hydrogen** (Lyman series, *n* = 1 → ∞) ≈ **1312 kJ/mol**.
