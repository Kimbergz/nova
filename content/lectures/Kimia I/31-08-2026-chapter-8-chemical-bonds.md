# Chapter 8: Chemical Bonds

**Outline:** 8.1 Lewis Structures of Atoms · 8.2 The Octet Rule · 8.3 Ionic Bonding · 8.4 Covalent Bonding · 8.5 Drawing Lewis Structures of Molecules & Polyatomic Ions · 8.6 Limitations of the Octet Rule · 8.7 Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bond · 8.8 Formal Charge · 8.9 Resonance Structures · 8.10 Metallic Bond

> Every substance around you — the water you drink, the salt on your food, the aluminium foil in your kitchen — is held together by just three kinds of chemical bond. Predicting which bond forms, and what shape the resulting molecule takes, starts with a simple picture: the **Lewis structure**, a map of every atom's valence electrons. This chapter builds that picture up from single atoms to full molecules and ions, then uses it to explain why metals conduct electricity and bend without breaking.

---

## 8.1 Lewis Structures of Atoms

A **Lewis structure** represents an atom singly, or in combination with other atoms in a molecule; it shows how the **valence electrons** are arranged among the atoms.

**Principles:**

1. Write the chemical symbol of the atom. Valence electrons are shown as **dots** placed around the symbol.
2. Except for **helium**, the number of valence electrons of an atom equals its **group number** in the periodic table.
3. The position of a dot around the symbol is not fixed — dots can go on any of the four sides. The first 1–4 valence electrons are arranged as **single dots**; once there are more than 4, electrons **begin to pair up**.

**How to draw the Lewis structure of an atom (worked example: lithium, Li):**

| Step | Action | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the valence electrons of the atom | Li (Z = 3): 1s²2s¹ → 1 valence electron (2s¹) |
| 2 | Write the chemical symbol | **Li** |
| 3 | Arrange the electron(s) around the symbol | **Li •** |

**Metals vs non-metals** — the number of unpaired (single-dot) electrons tells you:

- The number of electrons a **metal** atom will **lose**.
- The number of electrons a **non-metal** atom will **gain**.
- The number of **covalent bonds** a non-metal atom will usually form.

## 8.2 The Octet Rule

> The **octet rule** states that atoms of the representative elements form bonds so as to obtain **eight valence electrons** (an octet).

- In forming either an ionic or a covalent bond, atoms adjust their valence electrons so that each ends up with the electron configuration of a **noble gas**.
- Noble gases have a particularly stable octet of 8 valence electrons — **except helium**, which is stable with just 2 electrons.
- Atoms reach a noble-gas configuration in one of two ways:

| Mechanism | Result |
|---|---|
| **Transfer** of electrons | Ionic bond — e.g. Na• + :C̈l· → Na⁺ [:C̈l:]⁻ |
| **Sharing** of electrons | Covalent bond — e.g. H–Ö–H (water) |

## 8.3 Ionic Bonding

**Definition:** a chemical link between two ions, caused by the electrostatic force between **oppositely charged ions**. Also called an **electrovalent bond**.

**Formation:**

- An ionic bond forms by **transferring** one or more valence electrons from one atom to the valence shell of another atom.
- The atom that **loses** electron(s) — typically a **metal** — becomes a **positively** charged ion (cation).
- The atom that **gains** electron(s) — typically a **non-metal** — becomes a **negatively** charged ion (anion).
- The resulting compound is called an **ionic compound**.

**Example 8.1 — formation of NaCl:**

1. Sodium loses its one valence electron, forming the sodium ion (positively charged):
   Na• → Na⁺ + e⁻
2. Chlorine gains that electron, completing its octet and forming a chloride ion (negatively charged):
   :C̈l• + e⁻ → [:C̈l:]⁻
3. Overall Lewis representation: **Na⁺ [:C̈l:]⁻**

**Example (extra) — formation of MgCl₂:**

1. Mg atom loses **two** valence electrons to form the Mg²⁺ ion:
   •Mg• → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
2. Each of **two** Cl atoms gains one electron to form a Cl⁻ ion and achieve an octet:
   :C̈l• + e⁻ → [:C̈l:]⁻
3. Overall Lewis representation: **[Mg]²⁺ [:C̈l:]⁻₂** (one Mg²⁺ ion balanced by two Cl⁻ ions)

## 8.4 Covalent Bonding

**Definition:** a bond in which two atoms **share a pair of electrons**.

**Formation:**

- Atoms in a covalent bond are held together by the **electrostatic attraction** between the shared electrons and the nuclei of the atoms involved.
- Atoms bonded through covalent bonds form a **molecule**.
- Commonly, **non-metals** react with each other to form **covalent compounds**.
- A Lewis structure can represent covalent bond formation as a **single**, **double**, or **triple** bond.

### Types of bonding pairs

| Bond type | Electron pairs shared | Example | Lewis structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single bond | 1 pair | H₂ | H–H (or H:H) |
| Double bond | 2 pairs | O₂ | O=O |
| Triple bond | 3 pairs | N₂ | N≡N |

Other worked examples: **CO₂** has two double bonds (O=C=O); **HCOOH** (formic acid) has three single bonds and one double bond.

### Bonding pair vs lone pair electrons

- **Bonding pair electron**: the two electrons that form a covalent bond (a *shared* electron pair).
- **Lone pair electron**: a pair of valence electrons *not* involved in bonding (an *unshared* electron pair).

In H–Ö–H (water), the two O–H bonds contain the bonding pairs, while the two pairs of dots on oxygen are lone pairs.

## 8.5 Drawing Lewis Structures of Molecules and Polyatomic Ions

**Step 1 — Determine the total number of valence electrons** in the molecule or ion:

- Sum the valence electrons of each atom.
- For an **anion**, **add** one electron for each negative charge.
- For a **cation**, **subtract** one electron for each positive charge.

| Species | Calculation | Total |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | H: 2 × 1 = 2e⁻; O: 1 × 6 = 6e⁻ | 2 + 6 = **8e⁻** |
| NO₃⁻ | N: 1 × 5 = 5e⁻; O: 3 × 6 = 18e⁻; + 1e⁻ (charge) | 5 + 18 + 1 = **24e⁻** |
| NH₄⁺ | N: 1 × 5 = 5e⁻; H: 4 × 1 = 4e⁻; − 1e⁻ (charge) | 5 + 4 − 1 = **8e⁻** |

**Step 2 — Arrange the atoms** to show specific connections:

- The **least electronegative** element is usually the **central atom**.
- **Terminal atoms** are typically hydrogen and fluorine (or other halogens).
- Draw the skeletal structure, then place one pair of electrons between each pair of neighbouring atoms to form a single bond.

**Step 3 — Complete the octets of the terminal atoms first** (two electrons for hydrogen, eight for everything else).

**Step 4 — Place any leftover electrons on the central atom** (some atoms can hold more than eight electrons).

**Step 5 — If the central atom still lacks an octet**, move a lone pair from a terminal atom to form a multiple bond (double or triple) to the central atom.

### Worked examples

**Example 8.2 — CCl₄:**
Total valence electrons: C (1 × 4 = 4e⁻) + Cl (4 × 7 = 28e⁻) = 32e⁻. After forming 4 single C–Cl bonds (using 8e⁻) and completing the octet on each Cl (24e⁻ remaining), the structure is:

```
        :Cl:
         |
:Cl — C — Cl:
         |
        :Cl:
```

**Example 8.3 — H₂O:** Total = 8e⁻. Two O–H single bonds use 4e⁻, leaving 4e⁻ as two lone pairs on O:

```
H — Ö — H
```

**Example 8.4 — NH₄⁺:** Total = 8e⁻, all used in four N–H single bonds (tetrahedral around N); the overall ion carries a **+1** charge:

```
      H         +
      |
H —  N — H
      |
      H
```

**Example 8.5 — CN⁻:** Total = 10e⁻. A single C–N bond does not give carbon an octet, so two lone pairs are converted into two extra shared pairs, giving a **triple bond**:

:C≡N: → **[:C≡N:]⁻**

## 8.6 Limitations of the Octet Rule

Not every stable molecule follows the octet rule exactly. There are three recognised exceptions.

### (a) Incomplete octet

- The central atom ends up with **fewer than 8** valence electrons.
- Typical elements: **Be, B and Al**.
- **Example 8.6 — BF₃:** B (1 × 3 = 3e⁻) + F (3 × 7 = 21e⁻) = 24e⁻ total. Boron ends up with only 6 electrons (3 single bonds, no lone pair), which is a stable — if octet-incomplete — structure.

### (b) Expanded octet

- The central atom ends up with **more than 8** valence electrons (10 or 12).
- Typical elements: those in **Period 3 or below** (Si, P, S, and beyond), which have accessible **d orbitals**.
- **Example 8.7 — PCl₅:** P (1 × 5 = 5e⁻) + Cl (5 × 7 = 35e⁻) = 40e⁻ total; phosphorus is surrounded by 5 bonding pairs (10 electrons).
- **Example 8.8 — SF₆:** S (1 × 6 = 6e⁻) + F (6 × 7 = 42e⁻) = 48e⁻ total; sulfur is surrounded by 6 bonding pairs (12 electrons).

### (c) Odd number of electrons

- The molecule contains an **odd number of valence electrons** (so at least one electron must be unpaired).
- Most odd-electron molecules have a central atom from an **odd-numbered group**, such as N (Group 15) or Cl (Group 17).
- Examples: nitrogen monoxide, **NO**, and nitrogen dioxide, **NO₂**.

## 8.7 Coordinate (Dative) Covalent Bond

**Definition:** a bond formed between two atoms where the **shared pair of electrons is donated by only one of the atoms**. Also called a **dative bond**.

**Formation:**

- One atom (the **donor**) donates an electron pair; the other atom (the **acceptor**) accepts it, forming a coordinate bond.
- The donor must have **at least one lone pair** in its valence shell.
- The acceptor must have a **vacant orbital** in its outer shell.
- Once formed, a dative bond behaves exactly like an ordinary covalent bond.

**Example 8.9 — formation of NH₄⁺:** the N atom in NH₃ has one lone pair. NH₃ donates this lone pair to an H⁺ ion (which has an empty 1*s* orbital), forming NH₄⁺ via a dative bond.

**Activity 5 — two more worked formations:**

- **H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺:** the O atom in H₂O has two lone pairs; it donates one lone pair to H⁺ (empty 1*s* orbital) to form the dative bond in H₃O⁺.
- **BF₃ + NH₃ → BF₃NH₃:** the N atom in NH₃ has one lone pair, which it donates to BF₃'s boron atom (partially filled 2*p* orbital), forming the dative bond in the adduct BF₃NH₃.

**Activity 6:** aluminium trichloride (AlCl₃) reacts with diethyl ether to form a new molecule; the coordinate covalent bond is the one formed between the ether's oxygen lone pair and the empty orbital on Al.

## 8.8 Formal Charge

**Definition:** the apparent (not proven "real") charge on a particular atom within a Lewis structure.

**Purpose:** used to determine the **most stable** ("most possible") Lewis structure among several candidates.

**Characteristics of the most stable Lewis structure:**

1. A formal charge **approaching, or equal to, zero** is ideal.
2. A **negative** formal charge should appear on the **most electronegative** atom.
3. The sum of the formal charges on every atom must equal the **overall charge** on the molecule or ion.

**Formula:**

> Formal charge = (valence electrons) − [(lone-pair electrons) + ½ × (bonding-pair electrons)]

which is equivalent to:

> Formal charge = (valence electrons) − (unshared electrons) − (number of bonds)

**Example — H₂O:** each H: 1 − 0 − 1 = 0. O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0. (All formal charges are zero, as expected for a stable structure.)

**Example 8.10 — CO₂:** comparing two possible structures:

- **Structure A** (O=C=O): every atom has formal charge = 0 (O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0; C: 4 − 0 − 4 = 0). → **Most stable structure**, since all formal charges are zero.
- **Structure B** (:O≡C–O:, one triple and one single bond): formal charges are −1, 0, +1. → Less stable, because the **positive** charge falls on the more electronegative oxygen atom.

**Example 8.11 — OCN⁻:** comparing two structures:

- **Structure A** ([:Ö=C=N̈:]⁻): formal charges 0, 0, −1. → **Most stable**, since it has more atoms at zero formal charge.
- **Structure B** ([:O≡C–N̈:]⁻): formal charges +1, 0, −2. → Less stable, since the +1 charge falls on the more electronegative atom.

## 8.9 Resonance Structures

**Definition:** two or more Lewis structures for a single molecule or ion, where **no single structure accurately represents the real molecule** on its own.

- Indicated by a **double-headed arrow** (↔) between the structures.
- A resonance structure alone cannot accurately depict the molecule; instead, the true structure is a **resonance hybrid**, in which some electrons are **delocalised** over the entire molecule (often drawn with dashed lines for the "in-between" bonds).

![Two equivalent Lewis resonance structures of the nitrate ion, NO3-, that together form its resonance hybrid](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Nitrate_ion_resonance_structures.png?width=600)
*Image: "Nitrate ion resonance structures" by Ben Mills (Benjah-bmm27), Wikimedia Commons (public domain).*

**Example — ozone, O₃:**

```
:Ö—Ö=Ö     ↔     Ö=Ö—Ö:
```

Resonance hybrid (dashed bonds show delocalisation): **O┄┄O┄┄O**

**Example 8.12 — carbonate ion, CO₃²⁻:**

Total valence electrons: C (1 × 4 = 4e⁻) + O (3 × 6 = 18e⁻) + 2e⁻ (charge) = **24e⁻**. After the skeletal structure uses 6e⁻ for three C–O single bonds, 18e⁻ remain to complete the terminal-oxygen octets — but this leaves carbon with only 6 electrons, so one O lone pair becomes a C=O double bond.

Three equivalent resonance structures result, each with the C=O double bond on a **different** oxygen:

```
   :Ö:                :O:                 :Ö:
    |                  ‖                   |
:Ö—C—Ö:   ↔     :Ö—C—Ö:    ↔      :Ö—C=Ö
```

In each structure, formal charges work out to 0 (C), 0 (the doubly-bonded O), and −1 (each singly-bonded O), and all three structures are equally valid — hence the resonance hybrid shows all three C–O bonds as equivalent, each with partial double-bond character.

## 8.10 Metallic Bond

**Definition:** the electrostatic interaction between **metal cations** (positively charged nuclei/cores) and their **delocalised (mobile) electrons** — i.e. the valence electrons.

- Metallic bonds form **between metal atoms**, which are arranged in very compact, orderly patterns.
- In metallic bonding, metal atoms **lose their valence electrons** into a delocalised "sea" of free-flowing electrons surrounding the individual atoms/ions.
- The atoms become positively charged ions (cations) as their electrons are lost and delocalised.
- These electrons are no longer confined to a single atom — instead they are **shared among all the cations**.

### The "Sea of Electrons" theory

- Metals contain a number of free electrons moving in the spaces among the atoms, much like the molecules of the ideal kinetic theory of gases.
- A solid metal can be pictured as a **lattice of cation spheres** immersed in a "liquid" of free electrons moving through the gaps between them — the **Drude–Lorentz theory** of metallic bonding.

### What determines the strength of a metallic bond?

1. The **number of electrons that become delocalised** from the metal.
2. The **charge of the cation** (metal).
3. The **size of the cation**.

A stronger metallic bond results from **more delocalised electrons**, which increases the effective nuclear charge (Z_eff) experienced on the cation — in effect making the cation smaller. Because metallic bonds are strong and require a great deal of energy to break, metals generally have **high melting and boiling points**.

### Properties explained by the electron-sea model

| Property | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Malleability & ductility** (*kelembutan* / *kemuluran*) | Metals can be hammered into sheets (malleability) and drawn into wires (ductility), because layers of cations can slide past each other without breaking the surrounding electron sea. |
| **Luster** (*kilauan*) | The bright shine of metals comes from the presence of delocalised, mobile electrons at the surface. |
| **Electrical conductivity** | When an electrical potential difference is applied across a metal, the free, mobile electrons in the metallic lattice move towards the positive electrode, carrying current. |

## Summary

- **Lewis structures** map an atom's or molecule's valence electrons as dots, following the **octet rule** — atoms bond to reach 8 valence electrons (2 for helium), either by transferring electrons (ionic) or sharing them (covalent).
- **Ionic bonding** (metal + non-metal) transfers electrons completely, forming oppositely charged ions held by electrostatic attraction — e.g. NaCl, MgCl₂.
- **Covalent bonding** (non-metal + non-metal) shares electron pairs as single, double or triple bonds; a 5-step method builds the Lewis structure of any molecule or polyatomic ion (H₂O, NH₄⁺, CN⁻, CCl₄, etc.).
- The octet rule has three exceptions: **incomplete octet** (Be, B, Al — e.g. BF₃), **expanded octet** (Period 3+ elements — e.g. PCl₅, SF₆), and **odd-electron species** (e.g. NO, NO₂).
- A **coordinate (dative) bond** is a covalent bond where only one atom donates the shared pair — e.g. NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺.
- **Formal charge** identifies the most stable Lewis structure among several candidates (ideal: all charges near zero, negative charge on the most electronegative atom) — used in **CO₂** and **OCN⁻**.
- **Resonance** occurs when a single Lewis structure cannot represent a molecule/ion accurately; the true structure is a hybrid of two or more equivalent structures (e.g. O₃, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻).
- **Metallic bonding** is the electrostatic attraction between metal cations and a delocalised "sea" of shared electrons, which explains metals' malleability, ductility, luster and electrical conductivity.
